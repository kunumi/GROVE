## Guidelines for Restructuring the Orders Table and Understanding the Query Cost

---

### 1. Recognise the problem as a normalization problem: redundancy and inconsistency must be removed

Treat the repeated customer data as a normalization defect. The goal of normalization is to remove redundancy and inconsistency from the tables before anything else is designed.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 7e1d2d7ec900739f27d1]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization and RDBMS Fundamentals/RDBMS Types and Database Normalization Fundamentals.md`)

---

### 2. Apply 1NF first: give every row a unique key and eliminate duplicate data

As a baseline, ensure each row is uniquely identified. In your case, give the `customers` table its own primary key so that customer data is stored exactly once and referenced from the orders table.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 3eed6046b349bfab40b2] … [SWEBOK excerpt efb5158c35c6d1dd0444]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization and RDBMS Fundamentals/Database Normalization Forms and De-normalization.md`)

---

### 3. Apply 2NF: move customer attributes to their own table by eliminating partial dependencies

Customer name and address depend only on the customer, not on the order itself. Split them into a separate `customers` table, with the orders table holding only a foreign-key reference to it. This is exactly what 2NF requires.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 6d0499536d85be18506b])."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization and RDBMS Fundamentals/Database Normalization Forms and De-normalization.md`)

---

### 4. Apply 3NF: also remove transitive dependencies within the customer or orders data

If, for example, the customer's city is determined by their ZIP code, that too is a transitive dependency that should be moved to its own table. 3NF requires that such chains of dependency are broken.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt e6b8f2d0590bc4f09048]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization Forms and Requirements.md`)

---

### 5. Aim for 3NF or BCNF as the practical stopping point

For most production databases it is not necessary to push beyond BCNF. Higher normal forms address progressively rarer dependency types and add further complexity.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 0795a6d5f9294e0525cc]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization Forms Fourth Through Sixth.md`)

---

### 6. Use the schema's key structure (primary key / foreign key) to link the decomposed tables

After splitting, the `orders` table holds a foreign key that references the primary key of the `customers` table. This is the standard relational mechanism for maintaining referential integrity across normalized tables.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt f8831ad4424a319c25a6]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Fundamentals and System Architecture/Database Schema Structure and Design.md`)

---

### 7. Accept that normalization increases the number of tables and raises query time

Splitting customer data out of the orders table is the correct structural fix, but it comes with a cost: queries that previously read one row must now join two tables, which increases processing time.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 403fa59657edba586592]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization and RDBMS Fundamentals/RDBMS Types and Database Normalization Fundamentals.md`)

---

### 8. Understand the challenges of *not* normalizing before choosing to avoid joins

Engineers should not skip normalization simply to avoid join cost without first studying what an unnormalized schema costs in correctness and maintainability.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 0505d9fbd7d8adf63a29]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Structured Query Language and Data Management Fundamentals.md`)

### 9. When query performance is critical, consider selective de-normalization

If profiling shows that the joins introduced by normalization are unacceptably slow for a specific access pattern, the SWEBOK explicitly sanctions re-introducing controlled redundancy as a targeted remedy.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 548a0049dac977c4a9e6]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization and RDBMS Fundamentals/Database Normalization Forms and De-normalization.md`)

**Summary of the restructuring:** Move the customer's name and address into a dedicated `customers` table (with its own primary key), replace those columns in `orders` with a foreign-key reference, and normalize to at least 3NF/BCNF. The price is that every query retrieving order-plus-customer data now requires a JOIN, which raises processing time — a cost the SWEBOK explicitly acknowledges and that may justify selective de-normalization only after the performance impact is confirmed.
