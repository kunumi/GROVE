## Guidelines for Writing Down Your Team's Recurring Workflow

The guidelines are grouped into two parts: **what the write-up must state** (content requirements) and **in what forms it can be written** (representation options).

---

### Part A — What the Write-Up Must State

**1. State the required inputs and the outputs produced by each step.**

Every step of the workflow is a process, and a process description must specify what goes in and what comes out. Because the output of one step is typically the input of the next, making this explicit is what enables the hand-off between people (or agents) to work reliably.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 07bf054d3831a0ade4cf]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Engineering Processes, Lifecycles, and Measurement/Software Processes and Life Cycle Management/Software Process Definition and Life Cycle Specification.md`)

---

**2. State the transforming activities that convert inputs into outputs.**

Beyond just listing what enters and exits, the write-up must describe the work done in between — the activities or tasks that actually transform the input into the output.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 47bd2c965711c92d2c7a]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Engineering Processes, Lifecycles, and Measurement/Software Engineering Processes and System Lifecycles/Software Engineering Process Definition and Structure/Software Process Definition and Components.md`)

---

**3. State any controls, constraints, and enabling mechanisms for each step.**

The write-up should capture directives and constraints that govern how each step is carried out, as well as the [SWEBOK excerpt 62d9501556d336a3c20e]) that make each step possible.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 8a02b8f495be0b31bbdf]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Engineering Processes, Lifecycles, and Measurement/Software Engineering Processes and System Lifecycles/Software Engineering Process Definition and Structure/Software Process Definition and Components.md`)

---

**4. Assign roles and responsibilities explicitly, and name who has authority for each activity.**

The write-up must make clear which person or organisational entity is responsible for each step, so that there is no confusion about who does what — whether the reader is a new developer or an automated agent.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 1d4a7b11d06ee0710388]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Maintenance and Configuration Management/Software Configuration Management Planning and Implementation/Software Configuration Management Planning and Implementation/SCM Organization, Responsibilities, Resources, and Schedules.md`)

---

**5. State the expected results of key activities so that compliance can be verified.**

The write-up should explain not just what to do, but what a correct result looks like, so that anyone — including an automated coding agent — can check that a step was completed correctly before passing work to the next person.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 399cd52c5115fe5a77d8]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Quality Assurance and Verification Standards/Quality Management Systems and Process Evaluation/Software Quality Management System Implementation and Evaluation.md`)

---

**6. State constraints associated with each process, not just the activities themselves.**

The specification of a workflow must include the constraints associated with each step (not just the happy-path activities), because constraints define the boundaries within which the step must be executed.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 6444b726aad5aae95c31]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Engineering Processes, Lifecycles, and Measurement/Software Processes and Life Cycle Management/Software Process Definition and Life Cycle Specification.md`)

---

**7. Write it so that it is easy to understand and correct — it must be useful for human communication.**

The process specification must be written so that people can communicate with one another using it, and so that it is easy to understand and correct, because it becomes the basis for coordination, measurement, assessment, and quality management.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 0b8669ad38df2f57153a]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Engineering Processes, Lifecycles, and Measurement/Software Processes and Life Cycle Management/Software Process Definition and Life Cycle Specification.md`)

---

**8. Write it with the [SWEBOK excerpt f3b0fb2862a4c219e85d] to be verified.**

A process documented without its intended user in mind risks being ignored or misapplied. The write-up should explicitly call out the checkpoints where quality controls apply.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 9310ce2116f59aec8e4c] [SWEBOK excerpt 71da1d56a095cdcb86f2]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Quality Assurance and Verification Standards/Quality Management Systems and Process Evaluation/Software Quality Management System Implementation and Evaluation.md`)

---

**9. Record the rationale for non-obvious decisions within the workflow.**

If the workflow contains steps whose reasons are only obvious to the two senior developers who know it today, those reasons must be written down. Without this, maintainers will be unable to safely modify the workflow later.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 7599a55dbdd51a176f9e] long-term maintainability." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Design Concerns, Documentation, and Modeling/Recording Design Rationale for System Maintainability.md`)

---

**10. Document relevant facts, significant risks, warnings, and critical procedures.**

The professional obligation of documentation means the write-up must include facts, significant risks and trade-offs, and must clearly identify warnings and critical procedures — not just the routine steps.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt a3955ae490c761a6e180] … Clear identification of warnings and critical procedures." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Professional Practice and Collaboration in Software Engineering/Professional Responsibility and Intellectual Property in Software Engineering/Documentation and Requirements Trade-off Management/Documentation Standards and Requirements for Software Engineers.md`)

---

**11. Make information accessible to everyone — communicate clearly in documents so information reaches all team members.**

The write-up's value is only realised if its contents can reach all current and future team members. This means writing so that it is accessible to anyone who needs it, not only specialists.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 6f70083a92471e0c281e]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Professional Practice and Collaboration in Software Engineering/Collaboration, Communication, and Cognition in Software Engineering/Teamwork, Communication, and Professional Impact/Fair Responsibility and Clear Communication in Teams.md`)

### Part B — In What Forms the Write-Up Can Be Written

**12. Write it as a natural-language description (unstructured or structured prose).**

The simplest form is ordinary written language. It can be unstructured (a collection of "shall" statements) or structured (imposing a fixed sentence pattern such as actor–action, with an optional triggering event and condition), which improves precision and conciseness.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 73fdd84f59c4fe331ecf] … [SWEBOK excerpt 4eecb83f896611f6153d]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Requirements Specification and Validation Techniques/Natural Language Requirements Specifications/Structured Natural Language Requirements Specification.md`)

---

**13. Write it as a use-case specification.**

A use-case template structures the workflow around triggering events, parameters, pre-conditions ("requires"), guarantees ("guarantees"), normal and alternative courses, and exceptions. This format is particularly useful for capturing hand-offs between actors because it names the actor and the action explicitly.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 58831d3afbecba216f47] … Triggering event(s) … Parameters … Requires … Guarantees … Normal course … Alternative course(s) … Exceptions." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Requirements Specification and Validation Techniques/Requirement Specification Formats and Structures/Actor-Action Format in Requirements Statements.md`)

---

**14. Write it as a graphical process model (data-flow diagram, activity diagram, state chart, BPMN, Petri net, or IDEF0).**

Several standard graphical notations exist specifically for defining processes. Each captures a different aspect: data-flow diagrams show how data moves through process steps; activity diagrams show flow of control and concurrency; state charts show behaviour under events; BPMN and Petri nets are dedicated process-modelling notations.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 6b3ea1c4bdbe9b51c089]), [SWEBOK excerpt 2a7c513cf4547122d815])." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Engineering Processes, Lifecycles, and Measurement/Software Process Foundations and Measurement/Software Process Infrastructure Tools and Notations.md`)

---

**15. Write it as a formal or semi-formal specification.**

For steps that must be followed without ambiguity by automated agents, formal specification languages (such as Z, VDM, or SDL) provide precisely defined semantics that allow the specification to be mechanically analysed and are less likely to be misinterpreted.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 8f206e2a1a8e8cb14bf5]) … [SWEBOK excerpt 748acec10e907b192c82] … [SWEBOK excerpt b3d2801e465b37e10ad9]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Behavioral Models and Requirements Documentation/Behavioral Modeling Approaches and Formality Levels/Formal Modeling Methods for Requirements Specification.md`)

---

**16. Write it as an executable or model-driven specification.**

For automation purposes, the write-up can take the form of an executable model (e.g. in executable UML or a domain-specific language), which can be deployed directly in various software environments without change and can generate or drive automated agent behaviour.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 569e2c885316e4549505] [SWEBOK excerpt caaed29394dc10b3a8d6]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Construction Techniques and Practices/Fault Tolerance Techniques and Executable Models.md`)

---

**17. Choose the notation based on the audience and their concerns — not all audiences need the same form.**

No single notation suits all readers. New developers may benefit most from structured prose or use-case templates; automated agents may need formal or executable specifications; managers may need activity diagrams. The right choice depends on who needs the information and for what purpose.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 20cf988a91c40ee9914b] … [SWEBOK excerpt c91fc8cecfb1e869aeb7]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture_ Foundations, Views, and Patterns/Architectural Concerns, Views, and Viewpoints/Architecture Documentation and Stakeholder Views.md`)
