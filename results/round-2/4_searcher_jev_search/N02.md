## Guidelines for Evaluating a Proposed Multi-Service Architecture Before Implementation

---

### 1. Frame the evaluation against explicit stakeholder concerns — not just requirements documents

Before any analytical method is applied, map out every stakeholder's distinct concern (response time, hosting cost, modifiability, etc.). Evaluation must address not only those concerns individually but also **the consequences of their interactions**, because satisfying one concern (e.g., security) may make another (e.g., cost) harder to meet.

> "[SWEBOK excerpt 04f5c8c3ca12dfd9aaa2] easy-to-[SWEBOK excerpt 4bdd60ab71ec1b86d925]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations and Design Principles/Evaluating Software Architecture Quality and Fitness/Software Architecture Evaluation and Quality Tradeoffs.md`)

---

### 2. Gather Architecturally Significant Requirements (ASRs) through architecture analysis

Before judging the proposed decomposition, formally identify every requirement that **influences the architecture**: known requirements, stakeholder needs, and environmental constraints. Where the combination of requirements and constraints cannot be fully satisfied, use negotiation to resolve them before synthesis continues.

> "[SWEBOK excerpt bf2ef9e0191320e52934]'. … [SWEBOK excerpt 77685a0b0a70d90fbfa5]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations and Design Principles/Software Architecture Contexts, Methods, and Design Processes/Architecture Analysis and Synthesis/Architectural Design Analysis and Key Concerns.md`)

---

### 3. Apply the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method (ATAM) to reason about competing quality attributes

Use ATAM — or a comparable structured method such as SAAM or Quality Attribute Workshops (QAW) — to build a **utility tree** of quality attributes and define scenarios that illustrate them. The goal is to surface conflicts explicitly: a gain in one attribute (e.g., performance) may impose a cost in another (e.g., modifiability).

> "The Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method (ATAM) [SWEBOK excerpt 55bdeafc7cfabab1d90a])."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations and Design Principles/Evaluating Software Architecture Quality and Fitness/Software Architecture Evaluation and Quality Tradeoffs.md`)

---

### 4. Use compensatory multi-criteria decision techniques to score alternatives together

Because response time, hosting cost, and ease of change cannot all be cast as a single monetary figure, apply a **compensatory multi-attribute technique** (such as additive weighting, non-dimensional scaling, or the Analytic Hierarchy Process). These techniques allow a lower score on one criterion to be offset by a higher score on another, making the overall trade-off explicit and comparable.

> "[SWEBOK excerpt c3a972d37e2d72c35736] [SWEBOK excerpt d62e5ade89c458383496]) [SWEBOK excerpt d689423a7aa2ffb6ad23]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Engineering Decision-Making and Financial Analysis/Multi-Criteria Decision-Making and Intangible Assets/Compensatory and Non-Compensatory Decision-Making Techniques.md`)

---

### 5. Inspect the architecture from multiple views to expose concerns hidden in a single diagram

Create separate **architecture views** for the logical, process, physical (deployment), and development structures. Each view surfaces concerns that the others hide — for example, a deployment view reveals hosting-cost implications; a process view reveals runtime latency paths. Separating concerns by view prevents any one concern from dominating the evaluation.

> "[SWEBOK excerpt 8db6c1a9a1ae538920ac]. … [SWEBOK excerpt c23f0f89b4b1803d61de]) … [SWEBOK excerpt 821fb9ed55df1b1655d4]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations, Views, and Patterns/Architectural Concerns, Views, and Viewpoints/Architecture Views and Viewpoints.md`)

---

### 6. Use architecture viewpoints covering performance, availability, and deployment explicitly

Standard viewpoints exist for **performance, reliability, availability, communications, and deployment**, among others. Applying these named viewpoints ensures that each cross-cutting concern of the networked-service structure is examined systematically, using the established vocabulary of its discipline.

> "Common viewpoints include … [SWEBOK excerpt ae2d6aa938a98fddd9b6]. [SWEBOK excerpt 17b4d9b1daf8b8f138bf]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations, Views, and Patterns/Architectural Concerns, Views, and Viewpoints/Architecture Viewpoints and Their Applications.md`)

### 7. Conduct a structured architecture review — active or checklist-based — to assess risks

Hold an **architecture review** (formal or informal, internal or independent) before implementation. Rather than relying solely on passive reading, prefer *active reviews* in which each evaluation item requires the reviewer to perform a specific activity to obtain the needed information. The review should explicitly identify risks associated with each architecture concern.

> "[SWEBOK excerpt 2c4b3a63bfe3df0388ac]. [SWEBOK excerpt 1a2340a6ca6ae63ec11c], [SWEBOK excerpt 83424f8377e1ca6baddc]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations and Design Principles/Evaluating Software Architecture Quality and Fitness/Architecture Reviews and Metrics for Quality Assessment.md`)

---

### 8. Apply architecture metrics to quantify coupling, complexity, and change-responsiveness

Compute **quantitative metrics** on the proposed structure — component dependency, coupling and cohesion, cyclomatic complexity, and (for DevOps-oriented evaluation) lead time for changes, deployment frequency, and change failure rate. These metrics provide objective signals about ease of future change and operational cost.

> "[SWEBOK excerpt 0019d26a925ec7900503] … [SWEBOK excerpt d1ae0d7a64f64379e7b3]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations and Design Principles/Evaluating Software Architecture Quality and Fitness/Software Architecture Metrics and Evaluation.md`)

---

### 9. Apply simulation or formal design analysis to predict performance without building the system

Use **performance simulation** or **formal mathematical models** to predict response-time and resource-consumption properties of the networked-service structure. This allows the team to detect performance violations before any code is committed.

> "[SWEBOK excerpt 7c9848044a0924e23933]. … [SWEBOK excerpt e9370b60de6cf6bb205e])."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Design Quality Evaluation and Assessment/Design Analysis and Quality Metrics/Formal Analysis and Dynamic Evaluation Techniques.md`)

---

### 10. Trace every design element back to a requirement, and every requirement forward to a design element

Check the proposed service decomposition for **bidirectional traceability**: every service or component must correspond to at least one identified requirement, and every requirement must map to at least one design element. Missing mappings reveal either unsatisfied requirements or unnecessary components — both are risks before build.

> "'[SWEBOK excerpt 2d0df44bd61abfd79967] … [SWEBOK excerpt 66efc8c2f19a98a2dd85]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Requirements Validation and Management/Requirements Tracing and Bidirectional Traceability/Traceability Between Software Requirements and Design Elements.md`)

---

### 11. Document the rationale for the decomposition decision, including rejected alternatives

Explicitly record **why** the multi-service split was chosen over other structures, what alternatives were considered, what trade-offs were identified, and why alternatives were rejected. This prevents the team from inadvertently re-introducing a rejected option later and makes the technical debt of the current decision visible.

> "[SWEBOK excerpt a7e4160af8574fe372e0]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations, Views, and Patterns/Architectural Design Languages, Decisions, and Technical Debt/Documenting Architectural Decisions and Their Rationale.md`)

---

### 12. Watch for architectural technical debt introduced by deferred decisions

Any decision deferred now (e.g., "we'll fix the latency later") accumulates **architectural technical debt** that must eventually be paid — typically by future team members who did not create it. Evaluate the proposed structure explicitly for decisions that compromise future modifiability or evolvability before they are locked in.

> "[SWEBOK excerpt 50dabdf9f63fc60ee212]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations, Views, and Patterns/Architectural Design Languages, Decisions, and Technical Debt/Documenting Architectural Decisions and Their Rationale.md`)

---

### 13. Recognize and explicitly map the positive and negative interactions among quality-of-service constraints

Some quality attributes mutually reinforce each other (e.g., modifiability and reliability); others are in direct tension (e.g., speed and modifiability). The evaluation must **map these interactions** so that optimising for response time does not silently wreck maintainability, or vice versa.

> "[SWEBOK excerpt 80f0bcfa7533b7950700]. [SWEBOK excerpt 40a12e1196df62b83553]. … [SWEBOK excerpt e793447ee53bb020434f]."

(source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Requirements Engineering Viability, Verification, and Stakeholder Management/Performance Thresholds and Cost-Effective Quality Optimization/Cost-Effective Performance and Quality Trade-offs.md`)

---

**Summary.** The SWEBOK directs teams to: (1) identify all architecturally significant requirements and stakeholder concerns before evaluating; (2) use structured methods — ATAM, SAAM, QAW — to surface trade-offs via quality-attribute scenarios; (3) apply multiple architecture views and named viewpoints to expose each concern independently; (4) hold active architecture reviews and compute quantitative metrics; (5) use simulation or formal analysis to predict performance pre-build; (6) ensure bidirectional traceability; and (7) document rationale and technical debt so deferred choices remain visible. The SWEBOK does not, however, give specific numeric thresholds for response time or hosting cost benchmarks — those would need to be supplied by the project's own requirements.
