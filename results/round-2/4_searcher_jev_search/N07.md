## Guidelines for Handling the Unfamiliar API and Uncertain Performance Target

The guidelines are organized in two phases: **during planning**, then **while work is under way**.

---

### Phase 1 — During Planning

**1. Understand risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives, then build a risk register.**

Distinguish between uncertainty (lack of information — which describes your situation with the new API and unproven performance) and risk (the effect that uncertainty has on objectives). Formally identify, analyse, and prioritize each risk factor, then record all of them in a risk register.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt e2a2cb43fc578e221506]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Risk Management and Quality Assessment/Risk Management_ Identification, Analysis, and Mitigation Strategies.md`)

---

**2. Conduct a feasibility analysis before committing to the work.**

Before locking in scope, schedule, and budget, explicitly examine whether the performance target and the unfamiliar API are achievable within your constraints.

> (source: Feasibility Analysis is listed as a first step under "Initiation and Scope Definition" in the Software Engineering Management chapter: "1.2. Feasibility Analysis"
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Software Requirements Engineering Fundamentals/Guide to the Software Engineering Body of Knowledge V4/SWEBOK Guide Version 4.0 Overview/SWEBOK Guide Version 4.0a Table of Contents.md`, swebok-v4_table_11)

---

**3. Use a risk-driven architecture approach: address the most dangerous unknowns first in your architecture.**

Shape the architecture around the identified risks — the unfamiliar API and performance target are exactly the kind of "extremely demanding quality requirements" or "small solution space" scenarios this approach targets.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt c53642a9dd3b515e1f3d]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations and Design Principles/Software Architecture_ Foundations, Methods, and Modern Practice/Risk-Driven Software Architecture Approach.md`)

---

**4. At the architectural design stage, explicitly develop strategies to address performance as a crosscutting concern.**

Performance must be a first-class concern at the architecture level, not deferred to construction.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt f133816fdc3291e76ba8]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Software Architecture/Software Architecture Foundations and Design Principles/Software Design Principles and Processes/Software Development Stages and Design Architecture/Software Architecture Design and Computational Models.md`)

---

**5. Build a prototype early to explore the least-understood aspects — especially the third-party API and performance feasibility.**

Prototyping specifically targets the riskiest, least-understood parts first, the opposite of starting with what you already know.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt dd9033f7b0e5f3b3418e]."

**6. Treat prototype construction as a planned, monitored activity — not an ad hoc spike.**

A prototype should [SWEBOK excerpt c1aa6b55625e9848dc44], not a free-form experiment.

**7. Use simulation or formal design analysis to predict performance before committing to a design.**

Rather than relying entirely on testing later, use formal design analysis or performance simulation to validate performance properties early.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 7c9848044a0924e23933]… [SWEBOK excerpt e9370b60de6cf6bb205e])."

**8. Understand the economics of your performance target — distinguish the "fail point" from the "perfection point" and from the most cost-effective level.**

Performance targets are often arbitrary; understand the economic value curve. Even if the stated target cannot be fully met, the software may still have value. Also identify the minimum performance below which the project is not viable ("fail point").

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 58507504e5c6582e97d8]… [SWEBOK excerpt 71276f5618f7ce9e0752]."

**9. When planning, incorporate the magnitude of risk into how much planning effort you invest — plan the management of the plan itself.**

The amount of planning effort should be calibrated to the risks involved; do not plan lightly for a risky endeavour. The plan management itself must be planned.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 31428eabea66a8ec8aa2]."

**10. Assess the third-party API for risk, legality, and suitability — and introduce technical or procedural controls for external dependencies.**

Acquiring an external API is a form of software acquisition. Assess it for risk and suitability, and introduce controls to mitigate the exposure it creates.

Each dependency — including the API and its transitive dependencies — introduces potential risk and must be actively managed.

Do not assume an acquired component works correctly in your context; always verify completeness, correctness, and fitness for purpose in your intended environment.

### Phase 2 — While the Work Is Under Way

**13. Conduct risk management repeatedly throughout the project life cycle — not just at the start.**

Risk management is not a one-time planning activity. It must recur at regular intervals as the project progresses.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 9796a64f0b7b797d303d]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Risk Management and Quality Assessment/Continuous Risk and Quality Management/Risk Management Throughout Project Life Cycle.md`)

---

**14. Continually monitor the project's risk profile; recalculate risk exposures and act when thresholds are exceeded.**

Track the evolving risk picture throughout execution. When a risk exposure exceeds a predefined threshold, report it and respond.

When monitoring each task's outputs, do not treat risk review as optional — it is a mandatory part of the monitoring cycle.

**17. When control actions are taken, document and communicate all decisions, revise plans accordingly, and record all relevant data.**

The control loop is not just about taking action — it requires traceability: document what was decided, tell everyone affected, update the plans, and record the evidence.

**20. Conduct periodic performance reviews of project personnel and processes — not just the product — to detect problems with adherence to plans.**

Team and process performance reviews can surface signs of difficulty before they escalate into risk events.

Each phase or iteration is an opportunity to analyze what risks materialized, what worked, and what did not, so the whole organization learns.
