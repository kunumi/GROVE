## Kinds of Changes Made to Software Already in Use

The SWEBOK identifies the following categories of software maintenance — that is, types of changes applied to software that is already in use — and distinguishes each by its purpose and trigger.

---

### 1. Corrective Maintenance
**Guideline:** Treat corrective maintenance as the reactive category: its purpose is to fix faults and latent defects discovered in operational software. It is triggered by a failure or a defect that has been found, not by any desire to improve or extend functionality.

(source: "Correct faults and latent defects" — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md`)

---

### 2. Adaptive Maintenance
**Guideline:** Distinguish adaptive maintenance from corrective maintenance by its trigger: it is not driven by a defect, but by external change — specifically, changes in interfaced systems or infrastructure that the software must be updated to accommodate. The software may be working correctly, but changes in its environment make modification necessary.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt ebc3b37ef414b02f8fb9]" — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md`)

---

### 3. Perfective Maintenance
**Guideline:** Classify a change as perfective maintenance when it modifies a delivered software product to provide enhancements for users, improve program documentation, or recode [SWEBOK excerpt 3a7ea8de5455f72dc7ec] — but does **not** add large new functionality. The defining characteristic is improvement of what already exists rather than extension with new features.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt e5fe30859351837b8f04]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Types of Software Maintenance Activities.md`)

---

### 4. Additive Maintenance
**Guideline:** Distinguish additive maintenance from perfective maintenance on two grounds: ([SWEBOK excerpt fcfc18d62286edaf1445] quality attributes, and (b) it involves **[SWEBOK excerpt caf5bbddb4fe7a0a0663]. Where perfective maintenance refines existing capabilities, additive maintenance introduces capabilities that did not previously exist.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt f5ac630d0543a2ba3a3b]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Types of Software Maintenance Activities.md`)

---

### 5. Preventive Maintenance
**Guideline:** Treat preventive maintenance as proactive rather than reactive: it is performed not because a failure has occurred, but to prevent future problems — such as security threats — before they materialise. It differs from corrective maintenance in that no fault has yet been observed; and it differs from adaptive maintenance in that no external environment change has forced the update.

(source: "Prevent security threats" — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md`)

---

### 6. Emergency Maintenance
**Guideline:** Classify a change as emergency maintenance when it is an **[SWEBOK excerpt 446461437de2f594d2d3], with the understanding that proper corrective maintenance will follow. Its defining characteristics are that it is unplanned, it is a stop-gap measure, and it is explicitly pending a fuller corrective fix.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 3dca8108d3ba2c28d925]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Types of Software Maintenance Activities.md`)

---

### 7. Cross-cutting guideline: Avoid conflating categories in management reporting
**Guideline:** Keep the categories distinct in management and cost reporting. Grouping enhancements and corrections together creates the false impression that fault correction costs more than it actually does, and obscures where maintenance spending really goes — predominantly on enhancement and adaptation rather than defect fixing.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt c11d1ebfb3aaae1a3860]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md`)
