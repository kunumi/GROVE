# Types of Changes Made to Software Already in Use

Based on SWEBOK Guide V4, the following guidelines describe the kinds of changes made to operational software and what distinguishes each:

---

1. **Corrective maintenance addresses defects and latent flaws in the software.** It is performed to correct faults that cause the software to deviate from its intended behavior. This is reactive in nature — triggered by an observed failure or defect report.
(source: Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md)

2. **Perfective maintenance improves the software beyond its current functional baseline.** It is defined as "[SWEBOK excerpt 6e3893bacf78f98660e5]." It is distinguished by its focus on making the software better in ways not related to correcting defects.
(source: Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Types of Software Maintenance Activities.md)

3. **[SWEBOK excerpt 26010f1369f68a43db5f] it provides new functions or features.** Specifically, [SWEBOK excerpt 92805395f0c8c132b1f1]." While perfective maintenance typically improves existing capabilities, additive maintenance introduces capabilities that did not previously exist.
(source: Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Types of Software Maintenance Activities.md)

4. **Adaptive maintenance modifies the software to accommodate changes in its environment.** The need for adaptive maintenance arises when the software must be changed to work with new or changed interfaced systems, infrastructure, hardware, or other external dependencies — not because of a defect, but because the environment around the software has changed.
(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt ebc3b37ef414b02f8fb9]" — Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md)

5. **Preventive maintenance addresses future threats and obsolescence before they cause failures.** Its purpose is distinct in that it is forward-looking: it aims to "prevent security threats" and "[SWEBOK excerpt 75e12e5f36a78364dbb3]" rather than responding to a currently observed problem.
(source: Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md)

6. **Emergency maintenance is unscheduled and temporary by design.** It is defined as "[SWEBOK excerpt 29afddbf422c4d134083]." It is distinguished from corrective maintenance by its provisional nature — it is a stop-gap measure, not a final fix.
(source: Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Types of Software Maintenance Activities.md)

7. **The majority of maintenance effort goes to enhancement and adaptation, not defect correction.** "[SWEBOK excerpt 93bc56a512bddf06f5a6]." This challenges the common perception that maintenance is primarily about fixing bugs.
(source: Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md)

8. **Retirement of the software is itself a recognized maintenance outcome.** When no other maintenance category applies and the software has reached the end of its useful life, the appropriate action is to "retire the software." This distinguishes the end-of-life decision from ongoing change activities.
(source: Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md)
