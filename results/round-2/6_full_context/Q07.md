# Guidelines for Evaluating Test Suite Fault Detection Beyond Code Coverage

## 1. Use Mutation Testing to Measure Fault Detection Effectiveness
Create slightly modified versions (mutants) of the program and measure what percentage your tests can "kill." The mutation score — the ratio of killed mutants to generated mutants — is a direct measure of how well your suite detects faults. The higher the score, the stronger the suite's ability to discover real injected faults.
(source: `Testing Methodologies and Quality Assurance Practices/Software Testing Techniques, Measurement, and Management/Mutation Testing and Fault Injection Evaluation.md`)

## 2. Apply Fault Injection to Estimate Remaining Genuine Faults
Artificially introduce known faults before running the test suite. By observing how many injected faults are revealed, you can evaluate testing effectiveness and estimate the number of remaining genuine faults still lurking in the software.
(source: `Testing Methodologies and Quality Assurance Practices/Software Testing Techniques, Measurement, and Management/Mutation Testing and Fault Injection Evaluation.md`)

## 4. Use Reliability Growth Models to Predict Future Failure Probability
Collect failure data during testing and apply reliability growth models to estimate whether testing can be stopped or whether the software is mature enough for release. These models predict reliability based on observed failures and assume that fixing faults increases reliability over time.
(source: `Testing Methodologies and Quality Assurance Practices/Software Testing Techniques, Measurement, and Management/Reliability Growth Models and Test Evaluation.md`)

## 5. Track Fault Density and Fault Classification Statistics
Measure fault density — the number of faults [SWEBOK excerpt 0578196cf17abde6228c] classify faults by type. Knowing which types of faults occur most frequently (using taxonomies such as ODC) helps evaluate whether your suite is targeting the most likely fault categories.

## 6. Combine Functional and Structural Testing Techniques
No single technique is sufficient. Scenario-based (functional/black-box) and structure-based (white-box) techniques highlight different problems and use different information sources. Combining them improves overall fault detection effectiveness.
(source: `Testing Methodologies and Quality Assurance Practices/Software Testing Techniques, Measurement, and Management/Testing Approaches and Test Case Selection Methods/Combining Functional and Structural Testing Techniques.md`)

## 7. Apply Exploratory Testing to Catch Faults Missed by Formal Techniques
Exploratory testing — simultaneous learning, test design, and execution — dynamically adapts based on observed product behavior and can reveal faults that scripted techniques miss. It is particularly valuable where requirements are incomplete or informal.
(source: `Testing Methodologies and Quality Assurance Practices/Testing Approaches and Execution Strategies/Experience-Based Testing Techniques and Exploratory Testing.md`)

## 9. Assess Test Suite Completeness Against Thoroughness Measures
When deciding whether testing is sufficient, supplement coverage data with estimates of fault density, operational reliability, and cost/risk of remaining failures. Coverage alone is not sufficient as a stopping criterion.
(source: `Testing Methodologies and Quality Assurance Practices/Software Testing Techniques, Measurement, and Management/Test Completion and Knowledge Management/Test Completion and Artifact Reusability in Software Testing.md`)

## 10. Conduct Process-Level Evaluation via Defect Detection Percentage and Root Cause Analysis
Combine test phase reports with root-cause analysis to evaluate how effectively the test process finds faults as early as possible. Metrics such as defect detection percentage and residual risk help evaluate whether the suite is finding the right faults at the right time.
