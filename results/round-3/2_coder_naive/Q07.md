## Guidelines for Evaluating Test Suite Fault-Detection Quality (Beyond Code Coverage)

---

### 1. Use Mutation Testing (Mutation Score)
Measure your test suite's effectiveness by computing the **mutation score**: the ratio of "killed" mutants to the total number of generated mutants. [SWEBOK excerpt a47d64ce263982564d4e] software under test (SUT); if a test case detects the difference between the original and the mutant, the mutant is "killed." The underlying assumption — the *coupling effect* — is that tests designed to catch simple syntactic faults will also catch more complex, real faults.

> (source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt ae9ac78753f053c55ffe]."*)

---

### 2. Use Fault Injection
Deliberately introduce artificial faults into the SUT before running your test suite. By observing how many of those injected faults are revealed, you can evaluate testing effectiveness and estimate the remaining number of genuine faults in the system. Use this method carefully, as there is a risk of leaving injected faults in the codebase.

> (source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt b98a01ab11a6bb389235]."*)

---

### 3. Measure Fault Density
Evaluate the SUT's quality by tracking **fault density** — the ratio of discovered faults to SUT size. You can also look at richer metrics such as **fault depth** (the minimum number of fault removals needed to make the [SWEBOK excerpt cc3c948fabe72610fe33]).

Consult taxonomies and classifications of faults to understand *which types* of faults may exist in the SUT and how frequently they have occurred in the past. This knowledge can help you determine whether your test suite actually targets the fault categories most likely to appear, and can inform quality predictions and process improvement.

> (source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt 4bc5372472a04a0e0f08]."*)

### 5. Apply Reliability Growth Models
Use **reliability growth models** to gauge how well your test suite is improving the software's reliability over time. These models predict reliability based on observed failures and assume that fixing the faults causing observed failures increases reliability. Both failure-count and time-between-failure models exist for this purpose.

> (source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt b2e2e475396b666cf9aa]."*)

---

### 6. Apply Statistical Reliability Evaluation
Obtain a **statistical estimate of software reliability** from your test results to judge whether the SUT is mature enough for release, or whether testing should continue. This estimate gives a data-driven answer to the question "is the test suite good enough?"

> (source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt fa99d1606566f5056a0c]."*)

---

### 7. Compare Relative Effectiveness Across Techniques
Evaluate your test suite by comparing it against other techniques using **relative effectiveness** measures, [SWEBOK excerpt 7e598455b7fd6fa15448] faults found through testing to *all* [SWEBOK excerpt 89f26d2f4c5a1747fa0b]. This contextualizes how good your suite is compared to alternatives.

> (source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt c4ad890b5668c6dfc1ec]."*)

---

### 8. Track and Classify Defects Found During Testing
Establish a **defect classification taxonomy** to describe and categorize the anomalies found by your suite. Numbers alone may be insufficient to identify underlying causes. Recording and analyzing defect types helps reveal whether the test suite is exercising the right areas and can direct root-cause analysis.

> (source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt fd5ab336c195610f649e]."*)

---

### 9. Consider Fault-Based Testing Techniques to Fill Gaps
If your test suite does not specifically target known fault categories, supplement it with **fault-based testing techniques** that devise test cases explicitly to reveal likely or predefined fault categories. Tools like the Orthogonal Defect Classification (ODC) framework can focus test generation and reduce root-cause analysis effort.

> (source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt 9e7ece635a9472c2763e]."*)

---

### 10. Use Trend Analysis and Statistical Methods on Defect Data
Apply statistical and graphical analysis — such as Pareto analysis, control charts, run charts, and trend analysis — to defect data collected during testing. These help identify where problems concentrate, how well the test suite's detection capabilities are improving over time, and whether the defect discovery rate is plateauing (a sign the suite may be missing remaining faults).
