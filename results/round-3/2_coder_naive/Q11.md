## Kinds of Changes Made to Software Already in Use

SWEBOK's Software Maintenance Knowledge Area identifies **six categories** of changes (maintenance types) that can be made to software already in use. Each is distinguished by its *purpose* — what triggered the need for the change.

---

### 1. Corrective Maintenance
**Reactive modifications made to fix discovered faults.**

[SWEBOK excerpt f5658b7fbb60796ba696]. Corrective maintenance addresses problems that have already surfaced during operation. It is the type most people commonly associate with maintenance, though it actually accounts for a minority of total maintenance effort.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt f5658b7fbb60796ba696].")

---

### 2. Preventive Maintenance
**Proactive modifications to prevent future problems before they manifest.**

This type of change targets latent faults — problems that exist in the software but have not yet caused a failure. It is distinguished from corrective maintenance because no failure has occurred yet; the goal is to reduce the likelihood of one.

(source: "Correct faults and latent defects" listed as a maintenance purpose, alongside "One useful [SWEBOK excerpt c5a64a5d3ceb0605b391].")

---

### 3. Adaptive Maintenance
**Modifications to keep software usable as its environment changes.**

This category is triggered not by a defect but by changes in the external world around the software — such as new hardware, changed interfaces, or shifting organizational policies. The software itself may be functioning correctly; the environment has simply moved on.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt ebc3b37ef414b02f8fb9]" listed as a maintenance purpose in the Software Maintenance KA.)

Also: ISO/IEC/IEEE 14764 defines maintainability as covering "[SWEBOK excerpt d0bbecebc9df6758f7f7]."

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 198412adfdbbd81a628c].")

---

### 4. Perfective Maintenance
**Modifications to enhance or improve the software beyond its original design.**

Perfective maintenance is distinguished by its goal of improvement rather than repair or adaptation. It includes adding user-requested enhancements, improving documentation, and recoding to improve performance or maintainability. It does not add entirely new major features (that is the role of additive maintenance), but rather improves existing ones.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt e5fe30859351837b8f04].")

---

### 5. Additive Maintenance
**Large-scale additions of new functionality or features that significantly extend the software.**

Additive maintenance is a newer category distinguished from perfective maintenance on two grounds: ([SWEBOK excerpt 461f69c8d2d349041445] (not merely improving existing ones), and (b) the additions or changes are *relatively large*. Where perfective maintenance refines what is already there, additive maintenance substantively extends the software's capabilities.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt f5ac630d0543a2ba3a3b].")

---

### 6. Emergency Maintenance
**Unscheduled, urgent modifications to keep a system operational temporarily.**

This category is distinguished by its *unplanned* and *temporary* nature. It is not a permanent fix but a stop-gap measure intended to keep the system running until proper corrective maintenance can be performed.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 3dca8108d3ba2c28d925].")

---
