## Guidelines for Pinning Down What a Feature Must Do — Before Any Code Is Written

### 1. Treat ambiguity as a primary enemy from the start.
Recognise that your loose-prose request is already exhibiting SWEBOK's second primary requirements problem. [SWEBOK excerpt 51c7cc4da8e4b09a88a9]." Make eliminating that ambiguity an explicit goal of every workshop you run.
(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt a28cad0a9871cc13da8d]… [SWEBOK excerpt bac7168fd416bf652111]."*)

---

### 2. Perform a stakeholder analysis to identify everyone who must be in the room.
Before any session begins, identify all stakeholder *classes* — clients, customers, users, subject matter experts (SMEs), operations staff, QA professionals, and developers — who have a perspective on the feature. Missing a class means missing requirements. SWEBOK notes that "[SWEBOK excerpt c23cb107176251621f08]."
(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt 38d9165f4e53015c2c71]."*)

---

### 3. Run a facilitated workshop (JAD/JRP) with domain experts and the whole team.
Rather than gathering requirements in isolation, bring stakeholders together in a **joint application development (JAD)** or **joint requirements planning (JRP)** session. These are explicitly listed as elicitation techniques suited to converging on shared understanding.
(source: *"joint application development (JAD), joint requirements planning (JRP) and other facilitated workshops"*)

---

### 4. Treat elicitation as an active, not a passive, activity.
Do not simply ask stakeholders to describe the feature and accept what they say at face value. SWEBOK warns that "[SWEBOK excerpt a565547e5a57a0b4ab76]."
(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt 11007c66078e017f31d4]."*)

---

### 5. Apply the 5-Whys technique to uncover the true problem, not just a proposed solution.
When the product owner's prose reads like a solution ("build me X"), repeatedly ask "Why is this the requirement?" until you reach the real stakeholder need. SWEBOK explains: "The 5-whys technique [SWEBOK excerpt 716db07833277b41892e].'"
(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt eeada791e08cb7ee0327]."*)

---

### 6. Make each rewritten requirement unambiguous, testable, and atomic.
Once you have the true need, rewrite it to satisfy three of SWEBOK's core per-requirement properties:
- **Unambiguous** — interpretable in only one way.
- **Testable (quantified)** — compliance or non-compliance can be clearly demonstrated.
- **Atomic** — represents a single decision.

(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt 6eabf6d17bfa31257476]; … atomic, represent a single decision."*)

---

### 7. Use structured natural language (actor-action format or use-case template) rather than free prose.
Instead of loose paragraphs, impose structure: *Triggering event → Actor → Action → Condition/qualification*. This "goal is to increase precision and conciseness." For example: "[SWEBOK excerpt 4214c528e5ef7b2dccea]'."
(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt 4eecb83f896611f6153d]."*)

---

### 8. Write acceptance criteria using ATDD or BDD scenarios — before any design or construction.
Hold a **three-way meeting** of engineers, domain experts, and QA/test professionals to agree on concrete scenarios before coding begins. SWEBOK describes the ATDD process explicitly: "[SWEBOK excerpt 4a56a58a5f63b0b12a2d] [SWEBOK excerpt acf2737e4c17b2e47a7b]." Use BDD's **Given / When / Then** form for domain-expert friendliness.
(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt 4a56a58a5f63b0b12a2d] [SWEBOK excerpt acf2737e4c17b2e47a7b]."*)

---

### 9. Prefer acceptance-criteria language over natural language because "test case language is not ambiguous."
SWEBOK is explicit: "[SWEBOK excerpt 5a55e3be2e90db7068df]."
(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt 8c635b52f6833b7f34d2]."*)

---

### 10. Optionally escalate to model-based specification for the most critical or complex parts.
If BDD/ATDD scenarios still leave room for misreading, use **UML or SysML models** (structural or behavioral). "[SWEBOK excerpt fc0fbdedef0edd495391]."
(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt 243ec5ba82cc25e0ed3b]."*)

---

### 11. Conduct a multi-perspective review before sign-off.
After drafting the specification, have it reviewed by three distinct groups: (a) clients/customers/users to check that their needs are accurately represented; (b) requirements specialists to check clarity and standards conformance; and (c) developers/architects to check that it is sufficient to build from. SWEBOK recommends "review from multiple perspectives" and suggests using checklists or a "definition of done" to focus reviewers.
(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt a36fb4be0a88137baf40]… [SWEBOK excerpt 3029ae1319171a4f6a92]."*)

---

### 12. Ensure the agreed requirements use the stakeholders' own vocabulary and are acceptable to all.
A requirement that uses unfamiliar technical jargon will be reinterpreted by the domain experts who must approve it. Each requirement should "use stakeholder vocabulary" and "be acceptable to all stakeholders."
(source: *"[SWEBOK excerpt 70c6d682121f0181aaed]."*)

---

Following these twelve guidelines in sequence — from stakeholder analysis, through facilitated elicitation and 5-Whys analysis, to structured or scenario-based specification and multi-perspective review — will transform a loose prose description into a shared, unambiguous, testable definition of what the feature must do, agreed upon by domain experts and the team together, before a line of code is written.
