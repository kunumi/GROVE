## Guidelines for Pinning Down a Feature's Requirements with Domain Experts

### 1. Run a broad stakeholder analysis before writing a single requirement

Identify as many stakeholder classes as possible so that the requirements are not biased toward the loudest voices. This surfaces conflicts early and informs negotiation.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt f0b4c74eb9bf6d40ce93]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Stakeholder Identification and Requirements Elicitation Techniques/Stakeholders and Requirements Elicitation/Stakeholder Analysis for Balanced Requirements.md`)

---

### 2. Use facilitated workshops (JAD/JRP) to elicit requirements collaboratively

Bring engineers, the product owner, and domain experts into the same room (or virtual session) to discover and agree on requirements together, rather than passing prose back and forth.

> (source: "joint application development (JAD), joint requirements planning (JRP) and other facilitated workshops" — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Stakeholder Identification and Requirements Elicitation Techniques/Stakeholder Elicitation Techniques and Requirements Sources.md`)

---

### 3. Treat elicitation as an active, not passive, process — many needs are tacit

Do not simply ask stakeholders what they want and transcribe their answer. Users may leave important information unstated or have difficulty describing their tasks. The engineer must actively probe to surface hidden needs.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 794a6ba38ecb16fcd318]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Stakeholder Identification and Requirements Elicitation Techniques/Stakeholder Elicitation Techniques and Requirements Sources.md`)

---

### 4. Apply the 5-Whys technique to converge on the true problem, not a presumed solution

When a prose request describes a solution rather than a need, repeatedly ask "Why is this the requirement?" until the stakeholder's real problem is uncovered. This prevents building the wrong thing.

> (source: "The 5-whys technique [SWEBOK excerpt 716db07833277b41892e].'" — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Requirements Analysis and Refinement/Software Requirements Properties and Quality Criteria.md`)

---

### 5. Write requirements in structured natural language using an actor-action format

Replace free prose with a constrained sentence pattern — actor + triggering event + action + optional condition — to increase precision and leave less room for misreading.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt bf9e08ce5a0ee7193f9e]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Requirements Specification and Validation Techniques/Natural Language Requirements Specifications/Structured Natural Language Requirements Specification.md`)

---

### 6. Express the feature as a user story and expand it into concrete BDD scenarios

Capture intent with "[SWEBOK excerpt c6d4817748ea3e83ecf7]\>", then elaborate every scenario as "Given \<context\>, when \<stimulus\>, then \<outcome\>". This format is precise and readable by domain experts.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt fda42cf7467dfa8b04cb]… [SWEBOK excerpt cc69796e0dbe713af1b4]\>.' … [SWEBOK excerpt af60c2c4f2f9581370e9]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Requirements Specification and Validation Techniques/Test-Driven Development and Requirements Specification/Test Cases, Requirements, and Behavior-Driven Development.md`)

---

### 7. Agree on acceptance test cases with domain experts *before* any design or coding begins (ATDD)

Schedule a meeting where engineers, domain experts, and QA professionals jointly define the test cases that must pass to prove correct implementation — before writing a line of production code.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt cd5cc3b984affe631643] [SWEBOK excerpt acf2737e4c17b2e47a7b]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Requirements Specification and Validation Techniques/Test-Driven Development and Acceptance Testing/Acceptance Criteria-Based Requirements Specification.md`)

---

### 8. Write each requirement so it satisfies the individual quality properties: unambiguous, testable, atomic, and in stakeholder vocabulary

Check every statement against these properties. A requirement that cannot be verified, that bundles two decisions, or that uses technical jargon the customer does not recognise will be misread.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 6eabf6d17bfa31257476]; … atomic, represent a single decision; … [SWEBOK excerpt 825d8b93845096610f26]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Requirements Analysis and Refinement/Software Requirements Properties and Quality Criteria.md`)

---

### 9. Ensure the full set of requirements is complete, covering boundary and exception conditions

Ambiguity often hides in the gaps. The collection of requirements must [SWEBOK excerpt f9ddacc4f246107f3b90] — not just the happy path.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt b8010e9e221cc4cf004e]" — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Requirements Analysis and Refinement/Software Requirements Properties and Quality Criteria.md`)

---

### 10. Validate the requirements through multi-perspective reviews

Ask clients/users, specification-expert engineers, and the engineers who will build the feature to independently review the document, checking for errors, omissions, invalid assumptions, and lack of clarity. Provide a checklist or "definition of done" to focus reviewers.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt dfae591af3c0604a8a36] … [SWEBOK excerpt 6ff151b00fdca3243a39] … [SWEBOK excerpt eaaa1b058cb69abd033c]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Requirements Validation and Management/Requirements Validation Methods and Techniques.md`)

---

### 11. Build a prototype when textual descriptions remain unclear to non-technical stakeholders

If reviewers still dispute the meaning after written refinement, build a prototype (even low-fidelity) so that the engineer's interpretation is made concrete and stakeholders can confirm or correct it.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 992a1248872d1bd74de3]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Requirements Validation and Management/Prototyping for Requirements Validation and Feedback.md`)

---

### 12. For high-stakes features, use a formal or model-based notation to eliminate all natural-language ambiguity

Where the risk of misinterpretation is high, replace or supplement prose with a modeling language (UML, SysML, Z, etc.). The more formal the model, the less ambiguous it is.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 243ec5ba82cc25e0ed3b]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Requirements_ Foundations and Engineering Practices/Requirements Engineering_ Elicitation, Analysis, and Specification/Behavioral Models and Requirements Documentation/Behavioral Modeling Approaches and Formality Levels/Formal Modeling Methods for Requirements Specification.md`)
