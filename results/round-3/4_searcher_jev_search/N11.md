## Guidelines for Restructuring the Orders Table and Understanding the Query Cost

### Part 1 — How to Restructure the Tables

---

**Guideline 1 — Treat normalization as the systematic process for removing redundancy and inconsistency.**

Normalization is not an optional cleanup; it is the prescribed approach for addressing exactly what your orders table suffers from. Apply it deliberately before any structural decisions.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 7e1d2d7ec900739f27d1]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization and RDBMS Fundamentals/RDBMS Types and Database Normalization Fundamentals.md`)

---

**Guideline 2 — Apply First Normal Form (1NF): give every row a unique key and eliminate duplicated data.**

Your current orders table stores the customer name and address redundantly on every row. 1NF requires that each row be uniquely identified by a key and that duplication be removed. This is the foundation all higher forms build on.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 0288706213333bf1031a]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization and RDBMS Fundamentals/Database Normalization Forms and De-normalization.md`)

---

**Guideline 3 — Apply Second Normal Form (2NF): move customer attributes (name, address) to a separate Customers table, referenced by a foreign key.**

A customer's name and address depend only on the customer, not on the order. This is a partial dependency — a 2NF violation. The fix is to create a separate `Customers` table and have the `Orders` table reference it, rather than repeating the customer data inline.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 6d0499536d85be18506b])."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization and RDBMS Fundamentals/Database Normalization Forms and De-normalization.md`)

---

**Guideline 4 — Apply Third Normal Form (3NF) to remove transitive dependencies, and target 3NF or BCNF as your end goal.**

Beyond 2NF, check whether any non-key column depends on another non-key column (transitive dependency) and extract it too. The SWEBOK notes that most production databases are normalized to 3NF or BCNF — this is the practical target for your schema.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt e6b8f2d0590bc4f09048]. — [SWEBOK excerpt 45a40a03410154280eb6]). … [SWEBOK excerpt 4e8dc7c159181b00ce14]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization Forms Fourth Through Sixth.md`)

---

**Guideline 5 — If your goal is also to prevent insertion and deletion anomalies, consider Domain/Key Normal Form (DKNF).**

The DKNF (also called 6NF) is an alternative normal form specifically designed so that insertion and deletion anomalies cannot occur. The stale-address problem in your orders table is exactly a type of update anomaly; normalizing to DKNF would structurally prevent it.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 2431529db5b4d2f04959]."

**Guideline 6 — Prefer an RDBMS over flat/file-based storage, because RDBMS is designed to reduce redundancy and support concurrent, multi-table access.**

Moving to properly related tables is not just a schema exercise; it requires a data management system built to handle it. An RDBMS provides the relational links between `Customers` and `Orders` and significantly reduces data redundancy by design.

### Part 2 — The Query Cost of the Restructured Design

---

**Guideline 8 — Expect normalization to increase both the number of tables and query processing time.**

Splitting the customer data into its own table means every query that needs both order and customer fields must join the two tables. This is a direct, acknowledged cost: the SWEBOK explicitly states that normalization increases the number of tables and increases processing time.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt da4d8a3cc6d262f2b6b8]."

---

**Guideline 9 — When join cost becomes unacceptable, consider selective de-normalization — deliberately reintroducing redundancy for quicker data access.**

De-normalization is not a failure of design; it is an intentional, application-aware decision. If the join between `Orders` and `Customers` becomes a performance bottleneck, adding controlled redundancy back (e.g., caching the address on the order row for read-heavy workloads) is a recognized technique — but it must be weighed against the consistency risks you already experienced.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 548a0049dac977c4a9e6]."
> — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Data Systems, Networks, and Computational Foundations/Database Management and Network Communication Systems/Database Design and Normalization Fundamentals/Database Normalization and RDBMS Fundamentals/Database Normalization Forms and De-normalization.md`)

---

**Guideline 10 — Use SQL — including joins, views, and the right choice between static and dynamic SQL — to manage the increased complexity that normalization introduces.**

Once your schema is split across tables, SQL is the standard tool for joining them back together at query time. Views can present a pre-joined, customer-plus-order perspective without exposing the multi-table structure to every consumer. The choice between static (embedded) and dynamic SQL should be made by weighing the pros and cons for your specific workload.

### Summary

| Step | Action | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| 1NF | Assign a unique key; remove duplicate customer data from order rows | More rows/tables |
