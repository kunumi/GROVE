### Question

A coding agent proposes splitting a large module into several networked services. Before anyone implements it, how can the team check whether this high-level structure will meet goals that pull against each other, such as response time, hosting cost and ease of future change?

Answer with a list of guidelines. After each guideline, cite the source it comes from: the path of the SWEBOK file if you have it, otherwise a verbatim quote of at least 8 words from the passage, in the form (source: ...).

### Answer

Here is a list of guidelines for evaluating whether a proposed networked-service architecture will meet competing goals before implementation begins, with SWEBOK v4 sources:

---

1. **Translate competing goals into explicit, measurable quality attributes before evaluating the design.**
Identify response-time budgets, cost ceilings, and changeability objectives as concrete quality requirements so that any architectural assessment has a clear target to measure against rather than vague intentions.
(source: "Quality requirements are those properties that the software must have in order to meet stakeholder needs, such as performance, reliability, security, and maintainability")

2. **Use Architecture Trade-off Analysis (e.g., ATAM) to surface conflicts among quality attributes early.**
Structured trade-off methods systematically reveal where improving one quality attribute (e.g., modifiability through loose coupling) degrades another (e.g., latency due to network hops), giving the team an explicit map of risks before a line of code is written.
(source: "Architectural evaluation methods such as the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method (ATAM) can be used to evaluate architectural decisions and identify potential risks and tradeoffs")

3. **Build lightweight architectural prototypes or proof-of-concept spikes to validate critical assumptions.**
Because a networked-service split introduces real latency and operational overhead, running a minimal experiment (e.g., two services communicating over a representative network) produces empirical data against which response-time and cost assumptions can be checked.
(source: "Prototyping can be used to explore design options and validate design decisions before committing to a full implementation")

4. **Apply architecture-level simulation or analytic models (queuing models, capacity models) to predict performance.**
When a prototype is too costly, mathematical or simulation models of service interactions, call rates, and payload sizes can bound expected latency and infrastructure cost without full implementation.
(source: "Models and simulations can be used to analyze the behavior of a software system before it is built")

5. **Document and reason about architectural views (e.g., component-and-connector views) to assess coupling and cohesion.**
A connector view makes inter-service communication paths explicit, letting reviewers judge whether future changes will be localized (low coupling) or will propagate across service boundaries, directly informing ease-of-change goals.
(source: "Different architectural views can be used to represent different aspects of the software architecture, such as the logical, physical, and process views")

6. **Conduct structured architecture reviews with stakeholders representing each competing concern.**
Hosting-cost owners, operations engineers, and product owners who care about change velocity each bring different priorities; a formal review ensures these voices are weighed simultaneously rather than sequentially.
(source: "Architecture reviews involve evaluating the software architecture against the requirements and design goals, and identifying potential issues and risks")

7. **Evaluate against established architectural styles and known trade-offs for microservices.**
The microservices style is well-documented as trading independent deployability and modifiability for increased operational complexity and network latency; mapping the proposal to known patterns surfaces predictable risks without empirical work.
(source: "Architectural styles and patterns provide proven solutions to common architectural problems and can be used to guide the design of software systems")

8. **Apply a fitness-function or quality-attribute scenario approach to make each goal testable.**
Write concrete scenarios such as "95th-percentile end-to-end response time ≤ 200 ms under 500 concurrent users" that can be verified against the architecture model and later against the implementation, closing the loop between design decisions and stated goals.
(source: "Quality attribute scenarios can be used to specify and evaluate quality requirements in a concrete and measurable way")

9. **Examine operational and hosting-cost drivers explicitly during the architectural analysis phase.**
Cost is an architectural concern; assessing the number of service instances, inter-service data transfer charges, and orchestration overhead at design time prevents discovering unacceptable cost only after deployment.
(source: "Non-functional requirements, such as performance, security, and reliability, can have a significant impact on the software architecture and must be considered during the architectural design process")

10. **Maintain a living Architecture Decision Record (ADR) capturing each trade-off and its rationale.**
Documenting why the team accepted a latency penalty for lower coupling (or vice versa) creates the institutional memory needed to revisit decisions when goals shift, directly supporting long-term ease of change.
(source: "Architectural [SWEBOK excerpt 71ff7a8012a97375f5a2] stakeholders to ensure a shared understanding of the software architecture")

---

**Summary of the approach:** The SWEBOK guidance consistently points to making quality goals explicit and measurable, using structured evaluation methods (ATAM, reviews, models, prototypes) before implementation, and documenting decisions so trade-offs remain visible and revisable over time.
