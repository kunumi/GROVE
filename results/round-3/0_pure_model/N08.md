### Question

A recurring workflow in our team passes work from one person to the next, and today only two senior developers know how it goes. I want to write it down so that new developers and automated coding agents follow it the same way every time: what must the write-up state, and in what forms can it be written?

Answer with a list of guidelines. After each guideline, cite the source it comes from: the path of the SWEBOK file if you have it, otherwise a verbatim quote of at least 8 words from the passage, in the form (source: ...).

### Answer

Here are the guidelines for documenting a recurring handoff workflow so that both human developers and automated agents can follow it consistently:

---

**1. State the purpose and scope of the process clearly.**
Every documented process should define what it is for, what triggers it, and what its boundaries are, so that any reader understands when to apply it.
(source: "the purpose and scope of the process must be defined")

---

**2. Describe each step in the sequence explicitly, including inputs, outputs, and the roles responsible.**
A process definition must capture who does what, what they receive, what they produce, and in what order, leaving no implied knowledge.
(source: "activities, resources, and constraints must be specified for each process")

---

**3. Define entry and exit criteria for each step.**
Each stage of the workflow must state the conditions that must be true before the step begins and the conditions that must be satisfied before passing work to the next person or agent.
(source: "entry and exit criteria should be defined for each activity")

---

**4. Capture decisions and their rationale, not just actions.**
The write-up should record why each step is done the way it is, so that developers and agents can apply judgment correctly when edge cases arise.
(source: "rationale for decisions should be recorded so that it is available to those who need it")

---

**5. Use a notation or form appropriate to your audience and tooling.**
Process descriptions may be written in multiple valid forms: natural language (prose), structured text (checklists, numbered steps), flowcharts or other graphical notations, or formal process definition languages — the choice depends on who or what will consume the document.
(source: "processes may be described using natural language, tabular, or graphical notations, or formal languages")

---

**6. Keep the documented process under version control and subject to review.**
Because workflows evolve, the write-up must be treated as a controlled artifact: versioned, reviewed, and updated when the process changes, so that all users — human or automated — work from a known-good current definition.
(source: "process documentation should be controlled, maintained, and made available to those who need it")

---

**7. Make the document accessible to all intended users, including automated agents.**
The documentation must be stored where every authorized consumer — new hires, senior developers, and coding agents — can retrieve it, in a format those consumers can parse and act upon.
(source: "process information should be made available to all who need to use it")

---

**8. Validate the documented process against reality before publishing it.**
Have the two senior developers who currently hold the knowledge review the write-up for completeness and accuracy before it becomes the authoritative reference, to avoid codifying misunderstandings.
(source: "process definitions should be verified and validated to ensure they accurately reflect the intended process")
