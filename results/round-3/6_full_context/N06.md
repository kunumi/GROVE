### Question

At the end of each release our bug tracker tells us only how many bugs were found, and that count gives the team nothing to act on. What should we do with our bug records so that they help us introduce fewer bugs in later releases?

Answer with a list of guidelines. After each guideline, cite the source it comes from: the path of the SWEBOK file if you have it, otherwise a verbatim quote of at least 8 words from the passage, in the form (source: ...).

### Answer

Here are guidelines for making your bug records actionable for defect prevention:

1. **Establish a meaningful defect classification taxonomy.** Simply counting bugs is insufficient; categorize each defect by type (e.g., requirements error, design error, coding error) so you can identify which categories recur most frequently and target process improvements there. (source: Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Quality Assurance and Verification Standards/Software Quality Assurance and Defect Management/Software Quality Management and Defect Classification.md)

2. **Record not just counts but contextual data: when the defect was inserted, where it was found in the life cycle, and how much effort was needed to fix it.** This lets you compute cost-of-quality metrics such as defect density and failure rate and reason about where in the process defects originate. (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt b81ee849dfbed88b85ae]).")

3. **Develop defect profiles for your application domain.** Accumulate defect data across releases so you have a baseline (benchmark) showing which defect types are typical. Use those profiles to focus SQM effort where problems are most likely to occur in the next release. (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt b5c1600bb956ff935f32].")

4. **Perform root cause analysis (RCA) on the recorded defects.** Use techniques such as the 5-whys, cause-and-effect (Ishikawa) diagrams, or fault tree analysis to find why defects were introduced, not merely that they existed. [SWEBOK excerpt 70a1c443d896583a6c31]." (source: Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Quality Assurance and Verification Standards/Software Quality Assurance and Defect Management/Software Quality Management and Defect Classification.md)

5. **Feed defect data back into process improvement programs.** Use findings from RCA to implement corrective and preventive actions. Document and monitor those actions through an independent organization so the same root cause cannot recur unaddressed in future projects. (source: Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Quality Assurance and Verification Standards/Software Quality Assurance and Defect Management/Quality Metrics, Reliability Models, and Corrective Actions/Corrective and Preventive Actions in Quality Management.md)

6. **Use defect trend analysis and control charts to detect when the process is drifting.** Statistical techniques such as Pareto analysis, run charts, and control charts turn raw counts into visible trends that help decision-makers intervene before the next release ships. (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt fe94c53b907bb73f78db])")

7. **Apply orthogonal defect classification (ODC) or a similar semantic taxonomy.** Classifying defects semantically (e.g., by missing logic, wrong algorithm, interface error) allows you to infer which development or testing activity was inadequate and to reduce root-cause analysis time and effort. (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt edfad7bf64f9b3205797].")

8. **Link defect records to specific work products and life cycle stages so you can trace where each defect was introduced versus where it was detected.** This traceability reveals the cost growth of late detection and justifies investing in earlier reviews or tests. (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 09d3a128950f2e61ed96].")

9. **Use defect counts and density trends to build or calibrate reliability growth models.** These models predict the probability of future failures and can tell you when it is statistically justified to stop testing and ship, rather than releasing on a fixed calendar date. (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 5d8c08207647395b407d]… the product's reliability will increase.")

10. **Share defect data as part of a measurement program aligned to explicit organizational goals.** Measurements that do not support a specific decision are waste. Tie defect metrics to goals such as "reduce post-release failures by 20 %" so the team knows why they are recording data and what action a threshold crossing requires. (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 0935af8a54833f318869]… [SWEBOK excerpt 45b5e3b7b6c5b1b05700].")
