### Question

A test accepts a computed result when its difference from the expected value is below 0.001, which is far too strict for values in the millions and far too loose for values around 0.0001. How should the discrepancy be measured instead, and how does that measure tell how many leading digits of the result can be trusted?

Answer with a list of guidelines. After each guideline, cite the source it comes from: the path of the SWEBOK file if you have it, otherwise a verbatim quote of at least 8 words from the passage, in the form (source: ...).

### Answer

# Summary
The SWEBOK BoK defines **significant digits** as the leading (nonzero) digits of a computed result that agree with the true value, and shows through examples that the allowable error must be expressed relative to the magnitude of the value — not as a fixed absolute number. A fixed threshold like 0.001 implicitly gives far more or far fewer significant digits depending on the scale of the value being tested. The BoK also explicitly lists "Precision, Absolute Error, and Relative Error" as a dedicated topic alongside fixed-point arithmetic and numerical precision, confirming that relative error is the proper framework — though that file's content was not read within budget.

# Guidelines
- **Measure agreement in significant digits, not absolute difference.** The BoK defines significant digits as "[SWEBOK excerpt 37b39ccd54dea54d7cda]," making digit agreement the correct criterion for numerical correctness regardless of magnitude. (source: `root/Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Significant Digits in Measurements.md`)

- **The count of matching leading nonzero digits is exactly the number of trusted digits.** If k leading digits of the computed value match those of the true value, the result has k significant digits — this is the BoK's own definition of significant digits. (source: `root/Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Significant Digits in Measurements.md`)

- **Allowable error must scale with the magnitude of the value.** The BoK example shows ±0.5 mm on 15.5 mm yields 2 significant digits, while ±0.01 mm on 15.47 mm yields 3 significant digits — the tolerance is proportional to the value, not fixed. A fixed absolute tolerance of 0.001 would grant wildly different numbers of trusted digits for values of different orders of magnitude. (source: `root/Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Significant Digits in Measurements.md`)
