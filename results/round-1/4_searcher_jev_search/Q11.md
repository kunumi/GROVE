## Kinds of Changes Made to Software Already in Use

The SWEBOK identifies six distinct categories of software maintenance — that is, modifications made to a software product *after delivery*. Each category is distinguished by the **reason** that triggers the change.

---

### 1. Corrective Maintenance
**Guideline:** Use corrective maintenance to fix faults and latent defects discovered in operational software. This category is reactive: it is triggered by a problem that has already manifested in the deployed system.

(source: "Correct faults and latent defects" — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md`)

---

### 2. Adaptive Maintenance
**Guideline:** Use adaptive maintenance when the software must be changed to remain usable after its environment has changed — for example, when interfaced systems, infrastructure, or platform dependencies change. The software itself may be fault-free; it is the surrounding context that has shifted.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt ebc3b37ef414b02f8fb9]" — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md`)

---

### 3. Perfective Maintenance
**Guideline:** Use perfective maintenance to improve the software beyond its current correct operation — specifically to provide enhancements for users, improve program documentation, or recode to improve performance, maintainability, or other software attributes. It differs from additive maintenance in that its changes are smaller in scope and do not introduce entirely new functionality.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt e5fe30859351837b8f04]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Types of Software Maintenance Activities.md`)

---

### 4. Preventive Maintenance
**Guideline:** Use preventive maintenance proactively to prevent security threats and [SWEBOK excerpt 75e12e5f36a78364dbb3] before they become problems. The trigger is anticipated future risk, not a current fault or user request.

(source: "Prevent security threats" and "[SWEBOK excerpt 88f0df6eee63e400abe3]" — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md`)

---

### 5. Additive Maintenance
**Guideline:** Use additive maintenance when relatively large additions or entirely new functions or features must be incorporated into delivered software to improve its usability, performance, maintainability, or other quality attributes. Distinguish it from perfective maintenance, which covers smaller-scale improvements: additive maintenance involves new [SWEBOK excerpt a7421b3a40c80653ad92] after delivery.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt f5ac630d0543a2ba3a3b]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Types of Software Maintenance Activities.md`)

---

### 6. Emergency Maintenance
**Guideline:** Use emergency maintenance only as an *unscheduled*, temporary measure to keep a system operational when a critical problem strikes and cannot wait for a full corrective maintenance cycle. Emergency maintenance is not a substitute for corrective maintenance; it is a stopgap *pending* corrective maintenance.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 3dca8108d3ba2c28d925]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Types of Software Maintenance Activities.md`)

---

### Overarching Note on Proportions
**Guideline:** Do not assume that most maintenance effort is corrective. Studies show that over 80% of software maintenance is used for *enhancing and adapting* software, not for fixing faults. Grouping corrections with enhancements in management reports distorts this picture and leads to cost misattribution.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 0fcd2b20536e771d4fea]" — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Testing, Operations, and Maintenance/Software Operations and Maintenance Practices/Software Maintenance Roles and Cost Distribution/Software Maintenance Activities and Cost Distribution.md`)
