## Guidelines for Using Bug Records to Introduce Fewer Bugs in Later Releases

---

### 1. Always record defect data — do not let it be lost

Establish a systematic process to capture every error, defect, and failure found during reviews, testing, and operation. Data that is not recorded cannot be analysed or acted on.

### 2. Go beyond counts: classify every defect using a meaningful taxonomy

A bare count of bugs is insufficient. Establish a classification taxonomy that captures attributes such as type, origin, and cause so that patterns can be recognised and addressed.

> *"[SWEBOK excerpt cc445036fb1c8bc4d733]."*
> (source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Quality Assurance and Verification Standards/Software Quality Assurance and Defect Management/Quality Metrics, Reliability Models, and Corrective Actions/Corrective and Preventive Actions in Quality Management.md`)

> (source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Software Engineering Processes and Quality Assurance/Software Quality Assurance and Verification Standards/Software Quality Assurance and Defect Management/Software Quality Management and Defect Classification.md`)

### 3. Use fault taxonomies and historical frequency data to guide future testing

The testing literature offers well-established fault taxonomies (generic or quality-attribute-specific, such as security or usability). Use them together with the relative frequency of past faults to focus test design where defects are most likely.

> *"[SWEBOK excerpt 4bc5372472a04a0e0f08]."*
> (source: `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Testing Methodologies and Quality Assurance Practices/Software Testing Techniques, Measurement, and Management/SUT Measurements and Fault Classification for Test Planning.md`)

### 4. Compute richer quality measurements — not just totals — to understand trends and focus improvement effort

Replace raw counts with normalised and trend-aware metrics such as defect density (defects per unit size), error density, and failure rate. Apply statistical methods (Pareto analysis, control charts, run charts, scatter plots, trend analysis) to understand which defect types are most frequent and where problems cluster.

### 5. Build defect profiles and use them to guide future projects

Accumulate defect data across releases into an organisational measurement database. Use the resulting domain-specific defect profiles to predict where problems are most likely to occur in the next project and to calibrate readiness for release.

### 6. Prioritise which defect clusters to investigate using Pareto analysis and frequency-severity ranking

Not every defect type warrants the same investment. Use the "80/20 rule" (Pareto analysis) or frequency-severity prioritisation to pick the high-impact problems first, so that improvement effort yields the greatest return.

> *"[SWEBOK excerpt b8619cf04eec5a75c3e3]."*

### 7. Perform root cause analysis (RCA) to get from symptoms to underlying causes

For the prioritised defect clusters, apply RCA techniques — such as the 5-whys, Ishikawa (fishbone) diagrams, fault tree analysis (FTA), or failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA) — to identify why the defects were introduced, not merely that they existed.

Consider also using **Orthogonal Defect Classification (ODC)**, which collects semantic information about defects and is recognised as a valid methodology for reducing the time and effort of root cause analysis across a variety of development processes.

### 8. Translate root causes into documented corrective and preventive actions, then monitor them

For each identified root cause, select corrective actions that (a) prevent recurrence, (b) are within the organisation's control, (c) meet organisational goals, and (d) do not create new problems. Implement them, monitor their effectiveness, and report progress to relevant stakeholders.

### 9. Embed defect categorisation, detection, removal, and prevention into a continual improvement cycle

Quality improvement is not a one-off post-mortem; it is a feedback loop. Integrate defect data into an iterative improvement process (e.g., Plan-Do-Check-Act) covering life cycle process improvement, fault/defect categorisation, and personal process improvement.

> *"[SWEBOK excerpt 86bb2eed6e3dd20ff5f5] … ([SWEBOK excerpt 65477385ef73c1689d58]."*

### 10. Conduct post-release and post-iteration retrospectives, record lessons learned, and feed them back into the organisation's knowledge base

After each release (or iteration), analyse the quality and defect data, draw lessons learned, and store them in an organisational database so future teams can benefit.

Every defect metric you collect should be tied to a specific decision or action. Metrics that [SWEBOK excerpt 2ee3e8eb8ca2ed1e355a] produce but never used to support any decision waste time and energy.
