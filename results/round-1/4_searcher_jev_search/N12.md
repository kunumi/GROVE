## Guidelines for Measuring Numerical Discrepancy in Tests

### 1. Distinguish accuracy from precision before choosing your tolerance criterion.

**Accuracy** is what you care about when checking a test result: it is the closeness with which the computed value agrees with the true (expected) value. **Precision** is a different property — it [SWEBOK excerpt 0e2bbf1ebb51e2cfd867] computed values for the same thing agree with *each other*, not with the truth. A test tolerance should be an accuracy criterion, not a precision criterion.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt b586fdd39148fb34c49b]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Numerical Precision and Error Measurement/Fixed-Point Arithmetic and Numerical Precision.md`)

---

### 2. Use relative error, not absolute error, as the primary acceptance criterion.

The absolute error |x\* − x| is scale-dependent: a discrepancy of 1 is negligible for a result of 1,000,001 but catastrophic for a result of 11. The **relative error** — |x\* − x| / |x| — normalises the discrepancy to the magnitude of the expected value, making it meaningful across all scales. The SWEBOK explicitly states that relative error is "[SWEBOK excerpt f9903b61335b37d1ee1e]."

Your test should therefore accept a computed result x\* when |x\* − x| / |x| < ε for some chosen ε, rather than when |x\* − x| < 0.001.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt fa445030d08f0a9e79a4] … [SWEBOK excerpt 297dadd84958fa3cc461]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Numerical Precision and Error Measurement/Precision, Absolute Error, and Relative Error.md`)

---

### 3. Understand why a fixed absolute threshold fails at both extremes.

The SWEBOK's own worked example makes the problem concrete. The number 1,000,000 approximating 1,000,001 has an absolute error of **1** but a relative error of only **10⁻⁶** — the approximation is excellent. Conversely, 10 approximating 11 also has an absolute error of **1**, but a relative error of **0.1** — the approximation is poor. A fixed threshold of 0.001 would wrongly accept the first case if the gap were 999 (absolute error < 0.001 fails even earlier for large values) and would wrongly accept a small-value result that is orders of magnitude off.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 3baaec5a616a729ec42c]⁻⁶, [SWEBOK excerpt 8f40bc3ffa6de1a9ad8f]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Numerical Precision and Error Measurement/Precision, Absolute Error, and Relative Error.md`)

---

### 4. Note the special case: relative error is undefined when the expected value is zero.

Because relative error divides by |x|, it is undefined when x = 0. When the expected result is zero, fall back to absolute error or reformulate the problem (e.g., test against an expected non-zero neighbourhood). This is not an edge case to ignore; the SWEBOK states the undefined case explicitly.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt c7ee85f3eab02e26f163]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Numerical Precision and Error Measurement/Precision, Absolute Error, and Relative Error.md`)

---

### 5. Use the relative-error value to determine how many leading digits of the result are trustworthy.

A computed value x\* has **k significant decimal digits** if and only if its relative error satisfies:

> |x\* − x| / |x| < 5 × 10^(−k−1)

This criterion tells you directly how many leading (non-zero) digits of x\* match those of x, i.e., how many digits can be "trusted." For example, a relative error < 5 × 10⁻⁴ guarantees k = 3 significant digits; a relative error < 5 × 10⁻⁷ guarantees k = 6. Expressing the test tolerance as a target number of significant digits is therefore equivalent to, and more interpretable than, specifying a raw relative-error threshold.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 3ff0290952edc500cdf6])." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Numerical Precision and Error Measurement/Precision, Absolute Error, and Relative Error.md`)

---

### 6. Interpret significant digits as the leading digits of the approximation that are known to be correct.

Significant digits are not merely a formatting convention — they carry a precision guarantee. A result with k significant digits means [SWEBOK excerpt e64faa52e914aadc3322] non-zero [SWEBOK excerpt 850bb93ec9c915e6b9c3]. When a test checks for k significant digits, it is asserting that the computation is correct to k leading digits, regardless of the magnitude of the value.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 5f996de4eb1378c04b87] … [SWEBOK excerpt 2741fc0f4da1dd65f224]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Significant Digits in Measurements.md`)

---

### 7. Align the number of significant digits your test demands with the precision of the measurement instrument or computation method.

The SWEBOK illustrates this with a measurement analogy: a ruler gives 2 significant digits; a caliper gives 3–4. The same principle applies to numerical computation — the tolerance you demand must reflect the inherent precision of the algorithm or the data. Demanding more significant digits than the computation can deliver will cause spurious failures; demanding fewer will mask real errors.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt c93b1229717ccac60b3a]." — `/workspace/swebok-v4/root/Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Significant Digits in Measurements.md`)
