### Question

A test accepts a computed result when its difference from the expected value is below 0.001, which is far too strict for values in the millions and far too loose for values around 0.0001. How should the discrepancy be measured instead, and how does that measure tell how many leading digits of the result can be trusted?

# Guidelines for Measuring Numerical Discrepancy

1. **Use relative error instead of absolute error.** Absolute error (the raw difference |x\* − x|) is scale-dependent and therefore misleading: it makes an error of 1 look equally bad whether the true value is 1,000,001 or 11, even though the first case is far more accurate. The relative error, defined as |x\* − x| / |x|, normalises the discrepancy against the size of the true value and gives a scale-independent measure of accuracy.
(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 3baaec5a616a729ec42c]⁻⁶, [SWEBOK excerpt 642de4c87b3bc61128f0]." — Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Numerical Precision and Error Measurement/Precision, Absolute Error, and Relative Error.md)

2. **Define absolute error as |x\* − x| and relative error as the ratio of that absolute error to |x|, and ensure x ≠ 0 before computing the ratio.** When x = 0 the relative error is undefined, so a fallback strategy (such as using absolute error when the true value is at or near zero) is needed.
(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 7db6ed2ad33944ea1e8f]." — Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Numerical Precision and Error Measurement/Precision, Absolute Error, and Relative Error.md)

3. **Treat errors as non-negative quantities equal to zero only when the approximation is exact.** This convention prevents sign-dependent cancellations from masking real discrepancies and keeps acceptance thresholds unambiguous.
(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 292177e2ac03ab03be02]." — Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Numerical Precision and Error Measurement/Precision, Absolute Error, and Relative Error.md)

4. **Use the number of significant decimal digits (k) to express how many leading digits of the result can be trusted.** [SWEBOK excerpt 10bc11206db1b09772e4] when its relative error is less than 5 × 10^(−k−1), meaning the first k non-zero leading digits of x\* match those of x. Setting your acceptance threshold as a relative error bound directly translates to a guaranteed digit count.
(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt e98f68a3670db66a99c0]⁻ᵏ⁻¹. [SWEBOK excerpt 60ef169083bccf6d8b7d]." — Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Numerical Precision and Error Measurement/Precision, Absolute Error, and Relative Error.md)

5. **Understand the distinction between accuracy and precision before setting any threshold.** Accuracy is closeness to the true value; precision is repeatability or closeness among repeated measurements of the same quantity. A threshold expressed as a relative error bound addresses accuracy; a separate analysis may be needed to verify precision (consistency across runs).
(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt b586fdd39148fb34c49b]." — Foundations of Mathematics and Computational Systems/Numerical Methods, Algebra, and Calculus Foundations/Numerical Precision and Error Measurement/Fixed-Point Arithmetic and Numerical Precision.md)

6. **When a measurement method is chosen for a test oracle, verify that it is reliable (consistent) and valid (measuring what is intended).** A threshold criterion is only meaningful if the measurement method itself consistently and correctly captures the attribute of interest.
(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 59f4536decdb48f28ba7]… [SWEBOK excerpt bea873dd6838a4fa6afd]." — Engineering Decision-Making Through Data and Standards/Measurement Scales, Reliability, and Practical Application/Measurement Reliability and Decision-Driven Metrics/Reliability and Validity in Measurement Methods.md)

7. **Ensure every measurement supports a specific decision, and avoid collecting or applying metrics merely because they are easy to compute.** A fixed absolute threshold of 0.001 applied universally is an example of a metric not tied to any principled decision criterion; replacing it with a relative error bound tied to a required digit count gives the threshold genuine decision-making value.
(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 06557ae67be55e18f63b]." — Engineering Decision-Making Through Data and Standards/Measurement Scales, Reliability, and Practical Application/Measurement Reliability and Decision-Driven Metrics/Measurement Purpose and Avoiding Wasteful Metrics.md)
