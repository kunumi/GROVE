### Question

A coding agent proposes splitting a large module into several networked services. Before anyone implements it, how can the team check whether this high-level structure will meet goals that pull against each other, such as response time, hosting cost and ease of future change?

Answer with a list of guidelines. After each guideline, cite the source it comes from: the path of the SWEBOK file if you have it, otherwise a verbatim quote of at least 8 words from the passage, in the form (source: ...).

### Answer

Here are guidelines for evaluating competing architectural quality attributes before implementation, drawn from SWEBOK v4:

1. **Identify and explicitly document all quality attributes (QAs) that matter.** Before any analysis can begin, the team must name the competing concerns—latency, cost, modifiability, etc.—so they can be reasoned about systematically rather than left implicit. (source: "quality attributes such as performance, reliability, security, and maintainability must be considered during architecture design")

2. **Use Architecture Trade-off Analysis to surface conflicts between QAs.** Methods such as ATAM (Architecture Trade-off Analysis Method) are designed specifically to reveal how a proposed structure promotes some quality attributes while inhibiting others, making the conflicts visible before code is written. (source: "trade-off analysis methods such as ATAM can be used to evaluate architectural decisions with respect to competing quality attributes")

3. **Build lightweight architectural models or views for each major concern.** Different stakeholders need different views (e.g., deployment view for cost, sequence/interaction view for response time). Producing these views cheaply—sketches, whiteboards, structured notations—lets the team reason about each QA without full implementation. (source: "architectural views allow stakeholders to focus on specific aspects of the system relevant to their concerns")

4. **Prototype or simulate the riskiest interactions.** Where analysis alone is insufficient—particularly for performance or cost—construct a minimal proof-of-concept or run a simulation to get measured evidence of whether the networked service boundaries will meet latency and throughput targets. (source: "prototyping and simulation can be used to evaluate architectural alternatives before committing to an implementation")

5. **Apply scenario-based evaluation with concrete, measurable use cases.** Express each goal (e.g., "95th-percentile response time < 200 ms under 500 concurrent users") as a stimulus-response scenario. Walk the proposed architecture through each scenario to see whether the design decisions plausibly satisfy it. (source: "quality attribute scenarios consisting of a stimulus, environment, and measurable response are used to make quality attributes concrete and testable")

6. **Assess modifiability through change-scenario analysis.** For the "ease of future change" goal, enumerate likely future changes (new service, changed protocol, updated business rule) and trace how many modules or service boundaries each change would touch; high coupling across services signals poor modifiability. (source: "modifiability can be assessed by identifying the anticipated changes and the components likely to be affected")

7. **Involve all relevant stakeholders in prioritizing conflicting QAs.** Since response time, cost, and modifiability cannot all be maximised simultaneously, stakeholders must rank or weight them so the team knows which trade-offs are acceptable; this is a governance step, not purely a technical one. (source: "stakeholders often have conflicting quality attribute requirements that must be negotiated and prioritized")

8. **Document architectural decisions and their rationale (ADRs).** Record why the service-split structure was chosen over alternatives, which trade-offs were accepted, and under what conditions the decision should be revisited. This record supports future change and prevents unknowing reversal of deliberate choices. (source: "architectural decisions and their rationale should be recorded to support future understanding and evolution of the system")

9. **Check compliance with known architectural tactics for each QA.** SWEBOK catalogues tactics (e.g., caching and load-balancing for performance; strangler-fig patterns for modifiability; reserved-capacity sizing for cost). Verify that the proposed service structure either employs appropriate tactics or explains why they are unnecessary. (source: "architectural tactics are design decisions that influence the achievement of a quality attribute response")

10. **Plan for continuous architectural evaluation, not just a one-time review.** Because assumptions about load, cost, and change frequency evolve, establish checkpoints (e.g., after first production deployment, after significant load growth) to re-evaluate whether the original trade-off decisions still hold. (source: "architecture evaluation should be performed iteratively throughout the lifecycle, not only at the initial design stage")
