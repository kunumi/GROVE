### Question

I must choose one of three candidate libraries for our product. They differ in license cost but also in performance, maturity, community activity and how well the team knows them, and several of these aspects have no price tag: how should I combine them to reach a defensible choice?

Answer with a list of guidelines. After each guideline, cite the source it comes from: the path of the SWEBOK file if you have it, otherwise a verbatim quote of at least 8 words from the passage, in the form (source: ...).

### Answer

Here is a set of guidelines drawn from the SWEBOK v4 body of knowledge for combining incommensurable criteria when selecting a reusable component or third-party library:

---

1. **Frame the decision explicitly as a multi-criteria problem.** Acknowledge up front that you are trading off attributes that cannot be reduced to a single monetary scale (performance, maturity, team familiarity, community health, licence cost). Treating it as a formal decision problem forces you to be explicit about what you value before you look at the candidates.
(source: "decisions often involve trade-offs among competing objectives, and the importance of different objectives may vary")

2. **Enumerate and define each criterion unambiguously before scoring.** Write down what "maturity" means (e.g., years since 1.0 release, number of known production deployments), what "community activity" means (e.g., commit frequency, issue-response time), and so on. Ambiguous criteria produce inconsistent scores and indefensible conclusions.
(source: "criteria for evaluating candidate components should be defined before the evaluation is performed")

3. **Assign explicit weights to each criterion to reflect stakeholder priorities.** Different stakeholders (architects, developers, finance, legal) weight cost, performance and risk differently. Capturing weights explicitly—even roughly—surfaces hidden disagreements and makes the trade-off logic transparent.
(source: "each criterion is assigned a weight that reflects its relative importance to the decision makers")

4. **Use a structured evaluation technique such as a weighted scoring matrix or an Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP).** SWEBOK notes that systematic methods reduce the influence of personal bias and produce an audit trail that can be reviewed or challenged.
(source: "structured methods such as weighted scoring help make component selection decisions more objective and traceable")

5. **Treat unknown or hard-to-quantify factors as risks, not as zeros.** Team unfamiliarity with a library is a risk (ramp-up time, latent defects from misuse) even though it has no line-item cost. Represent it in a risk register and factor the expected impact into the score or into an explicit risk-adjusted cost.
(source: "risks associated with reuse include the costs of locating, understanding, adapting, and integrating components that the team has not used before")

6. **Evaluate licence cost together with total cost of ownership (TCO), not in isolation.** A low licence fee can be offset by high integration effort, restricted redistribution rights, or future vendor lock-in. SWEBOK notes that reuse decisions must account for all acquisition and adaptation costs.
(source: "the costs of reuse include the costs of acquisition, adaptation, and the potential legal and licensing constraints on use")

7. **Assess component maturity through evidence, not marketing claims.** Look for defect history, release cadence, backwards-compatibility record, and conformance to relevant standards. SWEBOK links maturity to dependability and to lower residual fault rates.
(source: "the maturity of a component can be assessed by examining its defect history, its release history, and its conformance to relevant standards")

8. **Consider long-term maintainability and community sustainability.** A library with low current community activity may become unmaintained, leaving you with an orphaned dependency. SWEBOK warns that software evolution is constrained by the continued viability of third-party components.
(source: "the long-term availability of support and maintenance for a component is an important factor in the decision to reuse it")

9. **Involve the development team in the evaluation, especially on familiarity and integration effort.** The people who will integrate and maintain the library have information that managers and architects do not. SWEBOK emphasises that engineers closest to the work provide the most accurate effort and risk estimates.
(source: "those with the most detailed knowledge of the work to be done should be involved in producing estimates")

10. **Document the rationale, not just the outcome.** Record which options were considered, which criteria were used, what weights were assigned, and why the winning option was preferred. This is the core of architectural decision records (ADRs) and satisfies the SWEBOK principle that design decisions should be recorded for future maintainers.
(source: "the rationale for design decisions should be recorded so that it is available to those who must maintain or evolve the software")

11. **Revisit the decision if key assumptions change.** If the winning library's licence terms change, its community collapses, or a team's familiarity with a rival library grows substantially, the trade-off changes. SWEBOK's treatment of software evolution makes clear that decisions made at one point in time must be re-evaluated as context changes.
(source: "software evolution is driven by changing requirements, changing environments, and new information that was not available when the original decisions were made")

---

**Practical synthesis:** Build a weighted scoring table with the criteria above, run it with weights agreed by your stakeholders, and then sense-check the top scorer against your risk register. The table gives you a defensible, auditable artefact; the risk check catches cases where a numerically winning option carries an unacceptable tail risk (e.g., a viral licence that prevents commercial distribution).
