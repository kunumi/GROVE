### Question

Beyond code coverage, how can I evaluate whether my test suite is good at detecting faults?

Answer with a list of guidelines. After each guideline, cite the source it comes from: the path of the SWEBOK file if you have it, otherwise a verbatim quote of at least 8 words from the passage, in the form (source: ...).

### Answer

Here is a consolidated list of guidelines, drawn entirely from SWEBOK v4, for evaluating whether your test suite is good at detecting faults — beyond code coverage:

---

### 1. Use Mutation Testing (Mutation Score)
Apply mutation testing and calculate the **mutation score** — the ratio of killed mutants to total generated mutants. A higher score indicates a stronger ability to find real faults. The underlying principle (the *coupling effect*) is that tests capable of catching simple, synthetic faults will also tend to catch more complex real ones.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt ae9ac78753f053c55ffe].")

---

### 2. Use Fault Injection
Artificially introduce faults into the system under test (SUT) before running your suite. By measuring how many of these injected faults are revealed, you can estimate testing effectiveness and even approximate the number of remaining genuine faults. Exercise caution: there is a real risk of leaving injected faults in the codebase.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt b98a01ab11a6bb389235].")

---

### 3. Measure Fault Density and Related Metrics
Evaluate the SUT by computing **fault density** (number of faults found divided by SUT size). Also consider richer metrics such as *fault depth* (the minimum number of fault removals needed to make the [SWEBOK excerpt c25c39f029a07db7b679]).

Track failures observed during test execution and use **reliability growth models** to predict the reliability trajectory of the SUT. These models assume that fixing observed failures increases reliability over time, and can help judge whether the test suite has exercised the SUT enough to reach a target reliability level.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt b2e2e475396b666cf9aa].")

---

### 5. Evaluate Relative Effectiveness Against Known Properties
Compare your test suite (or the technique used to generate it) against specific effectiveness properties: [SWEBOK excerpt 33d3ea6a918a951a7c76] faults found during testing to all [SWEBOK excerpt 51dec6781ee28ee17cdd] by the suite.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt c4ad890b5668c6dfc1ec].")

---

### 6. Know the Fault Types Your Suite Should Target
Study fault taxonomies and historical fault data for your domain. [SWEBOK excerpt d626790ac088b551641e] — then check whether your test suite exercises those fault categories.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 4bc5372472a04a0e0f08].")

---

### 7. Use Statistical Reliability Estimates to Judge Adequacy
Apply a **statistical estimate of software reliability** as a stopping/adequacy criterion. If the reliability estimate from testing does not yet meet the target, the test suite has not done enough to demonstrate fault-detecting power.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt fa99d1606566f5056a0c].")

---

### 8. Consider Fault-Based Testing Techniques to Design Better Tests
Augment your test suite using **fault-based techniques**, which [SWEBOK excerpt 9a636c5724b3735883bf]. Use fault models and frameworks like Orthogonal Defect Classification (ODC) to focus test case generation on the most likely faults.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 9e7ece635a9472c2763e].")

---

### 9. Track Defect Trends with Statistical Analysis
Apply statistical and graphical techniques — such as Pareto analysis, control charts, and trend analysis — to defect data collected during testing. This helps assess *how well detection techniques are working* and whether certain fault classes are being systematically missed.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 89bec3c7bdfc1df12b95].")
