## Kinds of Changes Made to Software Already in Use

SWEBOK's Software Maintenance Knowledge Area identifies **five primary categories** of software maintenance, each distinguished by its purpose and trigger. Here are the guidelines:

---

### 1. Corrective Maintenance — Fix faults discovered after delivery.
This is reactive modification to remove defects found in a delivered product. It addresses processing failures, performance failures, and failures to meet the specified design. It is distinct from other categories because it is driven by an actual failure or defect that was not present (or not known) at the time of delivery.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt f5658b7fbb60796ba696].")

---

### 2. Adaptive Maintenance — Modify software to keep pace with a changed environment.
This is [SWEBOK excerpt c137e304c4117aa9bd25] keep it usable in a changed or changing environment — for example, changes to the operating system, hardware, or other interfaced systems. It differs from corrective maintenance in that the software itself has no defect; the external environment has changed, forcing the software to be updated to remain operational.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt ebc3b37ef414b02f8fb9]")

---

### 3. Perfective Maintenance — Enhance the product to satisfy new or changed user needs.
Perfective maintenance is the [SWEBOK excerpt 6e3893bacf78f98660e5]. It is distinguished from corrective and adaptive maintenance because it is proactive and improvement-oriented — the software is not broken and the environment has not changed, but users want better functionality or quality.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt e5fe30859351837b8f04].")

---

### 4. Preventive Maintenance — Improve internal quality to prevent future failures.
Preventive maintenance involves modifying a software product after delivery to detect and correct latent faults before they become actual faults or failures. It is distinguished by being proactive and inward-looking — no failure has occurred yet and no new user need drives it; the goal is to forestall future defects by improving the internal condition of the code.

(source: "One useful [SWEBOK excerpt c5a64a5d3ceb0605b391].")

---

### 5. Additive Maintenance — Add substantial new functionality after delivery.
[SWEBOK excerpt 274b4d6b88ac7b6eff69]. The scale and scope of change is what sets it apart from perfective maintenance, which tends to involve smaller enhancements or improvements to existing functions.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt f5ac630d0543a2ba3a3b].")

---

### 6. Emergency Maintenance — Temporarily stabilize a system pending a proper fix.
Emergency maintenance is an [SWEBOK excerpt 29afddbf422c4d134083]. It is unique among the categories in that it is both unplanned and temporary — it is not a complete fix, but a stopgap measure taken urgently to restore some level of operation until full corrective work can be done.

(source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 3dca8108d3ba2c28d925].")

---

### Summary of Distinguishing Criteria

| Category | Planned? | Trigger | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Corrective** | Reactive | Known fault/failure | Remove the defect |
| **Adaptive** | Reactive/planned | Changed environment | Restore fitness for environment |
| **Perfective** | Proactive | User enhancement request | Improve usability/quality |
| **Preventive** | Proactive | Latent risk | Forestall future failures |
| **Additive** | Proactive | Need for large new capability | Add substantial new functionality |
