## Guidelines for Tracing Recurring Production Failures to Their Root Cause

---

### 1. Treat recurring failures as a symptom problem, not a patch problem — adopt formal Root Cause Analysis (RCA)

Do not stop at fixing the visible symptom. RCA is specifically designed for this situation: it identifies *why and how* an undesirable outcome happened, so the organization can eliminate the root cause rather than merely address immediately obvious symptoms.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 550d5f5bd799b70d8eb9].")

---

### 2. Establish a meaningful defect classification taxonomy before investigating

Track not just the number of incidents but also their type. Without classification, raw counts are insufficient to reveal underlying causes.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt fd5ab336c195610f649e].")

---

### 3. Record all defect and failure data — do not let it be lost

Data on errors, defects, and failures found during quality activities must be recorded; otherwise it cannot be used to find patterns or causal relationships.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 33d0041431e1e2706b8c].")

---

### 4. Conduct post-mortems after every incident

When an incident occurs, a formal post-mortem must be conducted to find the source and implement solutions that prevent similar incidents in the future.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 9b89c65485c19d20fd67].")

---

### 5. Use Problem Resolution with a multidisciplinary team, backed by monitoring, logging, and profiling

Investigating recurring production problems may require multiple disciplines and must be supported by observable evidence from the running system.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt baea92134ba15f083890].")

---

### 6. Implement product telemetry across all layers of the stack to detect and locate the problem source

Telemetry at the application, operating system, and infrastructure layers provides the foundation for identifying where a problem actually originates.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 31994c77504ec47e03e5].")

---

### 7. Apply the **5-Whys** technique to drill past symptoms to the underlying cause

Starting from the undesirable outcome, repeatedly ask "Why?" until the chain of causation leads to a root cause that, if fixed, breaks the cycle.

> (source: "The 5-whys technique … [SWEBOK excerpt e524a6814dc318ae160a].")

---

### 8. Apply **Change Analysis** to find what is different about failing situations vs. passing ones

Compare scenarios where the failure occurred against comparable scenarios where it did not. The root cause is likely to lie in the differences discovered.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 393788cc60b3b2c6fee3].")

---

### 9. Use **Cause-and-Effect (Ishikawa/Fishbone) Diagrams** to systematically explore contributing causes

Group potential causes into major categories (people, processes, tools, materials, measurements, environment) and decompose them level by level to identify what could have produced the failure.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 74539f2164ca00a5c989] [SWEBOK excerpt 3c498a2909e24b2781c5].")

---

### 10. Apply **Fault Tree Analysis (FTA)** when and/or relationships between causes matter

FTA is a more formal approach that explicitly captures whether a single cause or a *combination* of causes is required to produce the failure — precision that simpler diagrams cannot provide.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt d5015cc5504ac28a27a5] … [SWEBOK excerpt 7044f818121aac757c1d].")

---

### 11. Use **Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA)** to forward-chain from components that can fail

Where the previous techniques work backward from a known failure, FMEA works forward — starting from elements that can fail and tracing how they cascade into the undesirable outcome. This can expose failure modes that backward-chaining alone may miss.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt ceeb0db20b8cdc6ebe60].")

---

### 12. Follow the full RCA-to-improvement cycle: select, gather, identify, choose corrective action

A systematic process ensures RCA produces an actionable outcome, not just a diagnosis:
1. **Select** the high-priority problem (using Pareto analysis, frequency-severity prioritization, or statistical process control).
2. **Gather evidence** (testimony, standards, historical trends, experiments).
[SWEBOK excerpt aa9724bf8ba8b7df00e9].
4. **Select corrective actions** that prevent recurrence, stay within organizational control, meet organizational goals, and do not introduce new problems.

> (source: "Select the problem to solve … [SWEBOK excerpt 57489764f8955c5918d2]) … [SWEBOK excerpt 455f9fcce7eadc74eac2] … [SWEBOK excerpt dbe8929acdba854e2dfa].")

---

### 13. Evaluate and observe that the corrective action is actually working

After implementing the fix, monitor whether the problem truly stops recurring. Corrective actions should be observed to confirm their efficiency and effectiveness.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 3e734dd0354a666a32f0] … [SWEBOK excerpt 3b338484aea1dce0cd50].")

---

### 14. Document and track corrective actions through an independent organization

Corrective actions must be formally documented, submitted, and monitored — not left to individual teams — so the quality management system (QMS) is durably improved.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 55405e51ee9a56e7aa1d].")

---

### 15. Use quality measurement (trend analysis, Pareto analysis, defect density) to confirm whether recurrence stops

Quantitative measures — defect density, failure rate, trend charts — reveal whether the recurrence pattern truly ends after remediation and can guide where to focus further effort.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 89bec3c7bdfc1df12b95].")

---

**Summary:** The core insight from SWEBOK is that patching each occurrence addresses *symptoms*, while RCA — supported by telemetry, post-mortems, structured causal techniques (5-Whys, Ishikawa, FTA, FMEA), defect classification, and a full improvement cycle — addresses the *root cause*. Without the full cycle (diagnose → correct → verify → document), the same failure will keep returning.
