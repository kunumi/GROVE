## Guidelines for Making a Routine's Assumptions Explicit and Guarding Against Invalid Inputs

### 1. Define Preconditions for the Routine
Specify the conditions that *must* be satisfied before the routine executes. If these conditions are not met, the routine may produce erroneous results. These preconditions form part of the explicit contract between the routine and its callers.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt f6221f8cc5539f7cb39e].")

---

### 2. Define Postconditions for the Routine
Specify the conditions that the routine *guarantees* to be true after it executes successfully — including how parameters, data values, and return values will have changed. This makes the routine's output behavior explicit and verifiable.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 89a519aa5f387772eaf7].")

---

### 3. Identify and Document Invariants
Identify any conditions in the operational environment that must remain unchanged before and after the routine runs. These invariants [SWEBOK excerpt 110aa955b6681cc2455a] routine and should be made explicit.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 7caeb5a71ab6d4912de8].")

---

### 4. Apply Design by Contract
Adopt the design-by-contract approach, in which preconditions and postconditions are formally included in every routine. This creates a precise specification of the routine's semantics and clarifies its intended behavior for callers.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 0b7264f99dd70678ccd5].")

---

### 5. Use Assertions to Enforce Assumptions at Runtime
Place assertions — executable predicates — inside the routine to check that its assumptions hold during execution. Assertions are especially valuable for catching mismatched interface assumptions and for flushing out [SWEBOK excerpt e842886fd450a130cf66].

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 5911453b0a4f5e43261b]. [SWEBOK excerpt 5af2b51b24497cefec4c].")

---

### 6. Practice Defensive Programming: Check All Input Parameter Values
Protect the routine from being broken by invalid inputs by explicitly checking the values of every input parameter and deciding in advance how to handle bad inputs. Assertions are a common tool for this defensive check.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 798f1f64b64a6bf2581f].")

---

### 7. Handle Errors Explicitly With Appropriate Techniques
When an invalid or unexpected input is detected, handle it explicitly rather than silently. Options include returning a neutral value, substituting valid data, logging a warning, [SWEBOK excerpt 037e78d19fac2c7d7c91]. The choice of technique affects correctness and robustness.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 25fb1a8cce8efbace92b].")

---

### 8. Use Exception Handling to Signal and Respond to Bad Inputs
Use a throw/catch exception mechanism when a detected condition violates the routine's assumptions. Exception-handling policies should be carefully designed, including providing all relevant information in the exception message [SWEBOK excerpt 8fa6040b09c8e3f29c47].

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt aa95fcd1c82d33a12fdb] try-catch [SWEBOK excerpt 7bf944c609353bfb7687].")

---

### 9. Validate and Document All Assumptions; If They Cannot Be Validated, Document Them
Ensure that every assumption embedded in the routine is validated at runtime. If validation is not feasible, document the assumption explicitly for other developers — this is both a correctness measure and a security measure, since undocumented assumptions are attack vectors.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 0bcf94be1b9045b82f4d].")

---

### 10. Validate All Inputs (Security Perspective)
From a security standpoint, input validation should be treated as a top-priority practice. Failing to validate inputs is one of the most common sources of vulnerabilities.

> (source: CERT/CC's top 10 software security practices list the first practice as: "1. Validate input." — from the Software Security KA, section 4.4, Construction for Security.)

---

### 11. Apply Logical Inference / Formal Verification for Critical Routines
For high-assurance routines, use logical inference to specify preconditions and postconditions formally around each significant block, and use mathematical logic to prove they must hold under all inputs — allowing prediction of behavior without execution.

> (source: "[SWEBOK excerpt 27769f9cc03be9a38208].")
